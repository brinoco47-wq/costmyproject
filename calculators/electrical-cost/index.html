What this calculator estimates

This tool prices residential electrical work from the decisions that actually move the number: the project type (panel upgrade, whole-home rewiring, outlets and switches, EV charger install, ceiling fan install, or troubleshooting), the project-specific details (amperage, home size, circuit length, device counts), your home's square footage where it matters, and your region's labor rates. Electricians bill $100–$200 per hour in 2026 with a median near $135, and labor is usually 40–60% of any electrical job — so the calculator splits every result into materials, labor, and permit lines, applies your region's multiplier and optional ZIP-code adjustment, and shows a ±25% planning range. This is a budgeting estimate, not a quote: real bids move with panel condition, attic access, local permit fees, and whether your jurisdiction requires AFCI/GFCI upgrades on touched circuits.

How the math works

Every estimate above is built from three buckets — materials, labor, and permit — because that is how real electrician quotes are structured, and splitting them keeps the numbers honest when your market differs from the national average.

Materials (national prices, October 2026)

Material prices barely move by region: a 200-amp panel with breakers costs a contractor roughly $700–$1,100 wholesale anywhere in the US, a Level 2 EV charger unit runs $500–$800, and 12/2 Romex is priced by the foot nationally. The calculator holds materials at national prices and never scales them by ZIP — only labor moves.

Labor (regional + ZIP-adjusted)

Labor is 40–60% of most electrical jobs, so it gets the most care. The base rate assumes a $135/hr median electrician. Your region selection multiplies it (Rural 0.85×, South 1.0×, Midwest 0.95×, Northeast 1.15×, West coast 1.2×), and entering a ZIP refines it further with BLS wage data for your area — the same method documented in our labor-by-ZIP guide. A panel upgrade that prices at $2,800 in the South can easily be $3,400 in the Northeast for identical work.

What the NEC (electrical code) means for your bill

The National Electrical Code is updated every three years, and most states enforce the 2020 or 2023 edition in 2026. Three code realities hit homeowner budgets directly. First, AFCI protection is now required on nearly all 15- and 20-amp living-space circuits — when an electrician touches a bedroom or living-room circuit, the breaker must be upgraded to AFCI ($45–$75 per breaker vs. $8 for a standard one). Second, GFCI protection is required in kitchens, bathrooms, garages, outdoors, and laundry areas; a rewire or panel upgrade often triggers GFCI breakers across half the panel. Third, dedicated circuits are required for major appliances — that EV charger, the new induction range in your kitchen remodel, and the bathroom's heater-fan-light all need their own home runs. None of this is upselling; it is the inspector's checklist, and quotes that ignore it are quotes that fail inspection.

DIY vs. hiring an electrician

Swapping a like-for-like outlet, switch, or light fixture is legal for homeowners in most states and genuinely saves $150–$250 per device. Everything inside the panel — breaker swaps, new circuits, service upgrades — requires a licensed electrician and a permit in nearly every jurisdiction, and for good reason: the US Fire Administration attributes roughly 51,000 home fires a year to electrical failures. The smart middle path most pros recommend: hire the electrician for rough-in and panel work, then do your own device finish-out (installing the pretty outlets and switch plates) after inspection. Never work in a live panel, never skip the permit on service work, and if your home still has a Federal Pacific or Zinsco panel, replace it before anything else — both brands have documented breaker-failure rates far above modern panels.

Six money traps to avoid

  1. The unpermitted panel swap. A cash deal with no permit saves $300–$500 today and can void your homeowner's insurance or kill a sale when the buyer's inspector flags it. Always permit service work.
  2. Quoting the charger, forgetting the panel. EV charger quotes that skip the panel-capacity check are fantasy numbers — a full 100-amp panel turns a $1,500 charger into a $4,500 charger-plus-upgrade project.
  3. Per-device pricing without a trip fee disclosure. "$99 per outlet" ads often exclude the $150 service fee. Ask for the out-the-door total for your device count.
  4. Aluminum wiring "repairs" without COPALUM or AlumiConn. Old aluminum branch wiring needs listed connectors, not wire nuts and hope — improper splices are a top fire cause.
  5. Skipping the load calculation. Adding a hot tub, EV charger, or workshop subpanel without a NEC Article 220 load calc risks nuisance tripping or an undersized service. A real quote includes one.
  6. The cheapest bid with no license number. Verify the license with your state board — unlicensed electrical work has no permit path and no recourse if it fails.

Electrical terms worth knowing

Ampacity, load calculation, AFCI vs. GFCI, bus bar, subpanel, service mast, meter socket, grounding electrode, Romex (NM-B cable), conduit, junction box, dedicated circuit, double-tapped breaker, knob-and-tube — when a quote uses these terms, you now know what to ask about. Frequently asked questions

How much does an electrician cost per hour in 2026?

A licensed electrician charges $100–$200 per hour in 2026, with a national median near $135. Most also charge a service-call or trip fee of $75–$200 for the first visit. Apprentice-heavy shops run cheaper; master electricians and emergency calls run higher. Flat-rate pricing per task is common for small jobs like outlets and fans.

How much does it cost to upgrade an electrical panel to 200 amps?

Upgrading a panel from 100 to 200 amps costs $2,000–$4,000 in 2026, including the new panel, breakers, grounding and permit. Going to 400 amps (often two 200-amp panels) runs $4,500–$8,000. Add $300–$600 if the meter socket or service mast also needs replacement, and $300–$500 for the permit and utility coordination.

How much does it cost to rewire a house?

A full copper rewire costs $6–$12 per square foot in 2026 — roughly $8,000–$18,000 for a 1,500 sq ft home and $12,000–$30,000 for 2,500 sq ft. Knob-and-tube replacement adds 20–30% because every run must be fished through finished walls. Homes with open attics, basements or crawlspaces rewire cheaper than slab homes with no access.

How much does it cost to install an EV charger at home?

A Level 2 home EV charger install costs $1,000–$2,500 in 2026: $500–$800 for the charger unit plus $600–$1,700 for a dedicated 40–60 amp circuit, depending on panel distance and trenching. If your panel is full or only 100 amps, a panel upgrade ($2,000–$4,000) comes first. Some utilities offer $300–$1,000 rebates on charger installs.

Do I need a permit for electrical work?

Most jurisdictions require a permit for panel upgrades, new circuits, rewiring and EV chargers — permits run $100–$500 depending on scope. Simple like-for-like swaps (a new outlet where one existed, a ceiling fan on an existing box) often don't need one. Unpermitted panel or service work can void your homeowner's insurance and kill a home sale at inspection.

Can I do my own electrical work to save money?

Homeowners can legally do minor work (swapping outlets, switches, light fixtures) in most states, but panel work, new circuits and service upgrades require a licensed electrician and a permit almost everywhere. DIY electrical mistakes cause an estimated 51,000 home fires a year in the US. The safe savings play: do the cosmetic finish work yourself and hire a pro for anything inside the panel.

How much does it cost to install a ceiling fan?

Installing a ceiling fan costs $200–$500 in 2026 when a rated electrical box already exists. Adding a new fan-rated box and support where there was only a light fixture runs $350–$700. Vaulted or very high ceilings add $75–$150 for the downrod and extra labor. The fan itself is separate: $75–$400 depending on size and features.

Why do electricians charge a service call fee?

The $75–$200 service-call fee covers travel, diagnosis time and the business cost of rolling a stocked truck to your door — typically including the first 30–60 minutes of troubleshooting. Reputable shops credit the fee toward the repair if you approve the work. Always ask whether the fee is waived with repair before booking.
