What's included — and what isn't

✓ Included in every estimate

  • Paint, per your brand, price, and coats
  • Primer, when your surface condition calls for it
  • Supplies allowance (tape, rollers, drop cloths)
  • Pro labor, when you select "Hire a pro"
  • 10% waste & touch-up allowance, rounded up to whole gallons

✗ Not included

  • Drywall repair beyond minor patching
  • Lead-paint testing or remediation (pre-1978 homes)
  • Moving or storing furniture
  • Wallpaper removal, color consultation, design fees
  • Scaffolding/lift rental on multi-story exteriors (often billed separately)

If a contractor's quote covers items on the right-hand list and ours doesn't, that's expected — it's also exactly what you should ask them to itemize before comparing.

Surprise costs painters don't put in the first quote

The worked example covers walls, paint, supplies, and labor. These are the extras that most often appear after work starts — ask about each one before you sign:

  • Repairs first. Drywall cracks, water damage, and rotted trim are usually billed separately from painting. Get repairs priced in writing before painting starts, not discovered mid-job.
  • Primer for big color changes. Going dark-to-light (or the reverse) usually needs a primer coat. Primer runs $30/gal in our price table — one extra coat on a 12×15 room is roughly a gallon plus its labor.
  • Trim and doors. The worked example is walls only. Trim, doors, and built-ins are quoted separately in most bids — confirm whether your quote includes them.
  • High ceilings and stairwells. Anything above 8 ft slows the job and needs taller equipment. Our labor range ($1–$6/sq ft) exists largely because of this — tall walls land at the top of it.
  • Sheen and brand jumps. The table prices Behr Premium Plus at $43.98/gal; Sherwin-Williams SuperPaint runs $60–$77 and Benjamin Moore Regal Select $70–$78. Same room, different paint, different total.

Before you sign a painting quote

  1. Measure your own walls first (the 2-minute method above) so you can sanity-check the painter's square footage.
  2. Confirm the paint brand, line, and sheen in writing — not just "two coats."
  3. Ask whether primer, repairs, trim, and doors are included or separate.
  4. Get the prep scope named: caulking, sanding, and patching are where cheap bids cut corners.
  5. Confirm cleanup and touch-up terms before the crew leaves, not after.

Interior painting costs

The honest answer: $140–$300 DIY per average room, or $700–$1,100 hiring a pro — and labor, not paint, is almost the entire difference. Paint is cheap; hours are expensive. Labor runs 70–85% of a professional paint job. A gallon of good paint ($44) covers a whole bedroom — the other $800 in a pro quote is someone's skilled time, prep work, and overhead.

The per-square-foot trap

Some painters quote per paintable square foot, others per floor square foot, others per room. A $3/sq ft quote on floor area is roughly a $1/sq ft quote on wall area — always ask which basis. Prep work is the other hidden variable: patching drywall, sanding, caulking, and stain-blocking can double labor hours on a rough room, and two quotes that both say "paint bedroom" may assume wildly different prep. Ceiling height matters too: 9-ft and vaulted ceilings add area and slow the work (ladders, cutting in) — expect 15–30% more than the 8-ft baseline.

How long a room actually takes

Digital Estimating's 2026 guide times a pro crew at 2–3 hours for a bathroom, 4–5 hours for a bedroom, 5–7 hours for a living room, and 3–5 working days for a whole 2,000 sq ft interior with a two-to-three-person crew — add 1–2 hours per room for trim and baseboards. That is why a "one-day whole-house" quote deserves skepticism, and why DIY timelines stretch: the same bedroom that takes a crew half a day takes a weekend DIYer two days once drying time between coats is counted. Heavy prep, wallpaper removal, or a dark-to-light color change extends every figure above.

How to save on interior painting without regretting it

  • Buy 5-gallon buckets for big jobs — Behr Premium Plus drops from $43.98 to $39.20/gal in the 5-gal size at Home Depot.
  • Do your own prep. Moving furniture, removing outlet covers, and light patching yourself can shave real hours off a pro bid.
  • One color throughout cuts labor — no extra cutting-in, no leftover partial gallons.
  • Don't skip the second coat to save $44. A one-coat job that flashes and shows through costs a full repaint later.

What ceilings, trim, and doors add

The room figures above are walls only, and walls-only quotes are where most budget surprises start. Ceilings run $1–$2 per sq ft of ceiling area, interior doors $75–$150 each (both sides), baseboards and trim $1–$4 per linear foot, and crown molding $2–$6 per linear foot. On a 12×15 living room that turns a ~$1,038 walls-only pro job into roughly $1,700 with ceiling, trim, and two doors included — before any bundling discount. Exterior painting costs

For a typical US home: $600–$1,000 DIY, or $4,000–$8,000+ with a pro. The wide range isn't noise — it's stories, siding condition, and prep. Two bids for the same house can differ by 2× and both be "correct" — because one includes full prep (power wash, scrape, caulk, spot-prime, minor carpentry) and the other assumes your siding is already perfect. Always compare line items, not totals.

The four cost drivers

  1. Stories. A two-story home costs 25–50% more than the same square footage on one level — scaffolding or lift rental, slower brush/roller production up high, and more safety overhead.
  2. Siding condition. Prep is the swing factor: power washing, scraping failing paint, caulking gaps, and spot-priming bare wood can be half the labor on a 20-year-old house — and nearly zero on a 5-year-old one.
  3. Repairs. Rotted trim, split siding, and failed caulk get fixed before paint goes on. Budget a repair contingency; good contractors itemize it instead of hiding it.
  4. Paint spec. Exterior formulations run about 15–25% more per gallon than interior (HomeAdvisor 2026), and premium exterior lines earn it back in longer repaint cycles.

What's included in an exterior quote — and what isn't

✓ A complete exterior quote includes

  • Power washing before painting
  • Scraping failing paint and spot-priming bare wood
  • Caulking gaps around windows, doors, and trim
  • Two finish coats of exterior-rated paint
  • Protection for landscaping, windows, and fixtures

✗ Often billed separately (ask!)

  • Rotted wood / trim replacement (carpentry)
  • Lead-paint testing and remediation (pre-1978 homes)
  • Scaffolding or lift rental on 2+ stories
  • Gutter, shutter, or deck refinishing
  • Landscaping repair if plants get damaged

When one bid is far cheaper than the others, it's usually missing items from the left column — not a bargain, a scope gap. Get the scope in writing, then compare.

Exterior: DIY or pro?

DIY is realistic for single-story homes with sound siding: a long weekend with a sprayer or rollers, $500–$800 in materials, and no ladder heroics. Hire a pro for two stories — the fall risk, scaffolding logistics, and prep volume make it the rare job where professional equipment genuinely changes the outcome. Either way, never paint in direct hot sun or when rain is due within 24 hours; the can's temperature specs (usually 50–90°F) are real.

What drives the price up or down

Beyond the four cost drivers above, two factors swing the final number more than anything else: where you live and when you book.

Planning ranges, October 2026 — labor is the variable; paint and supplies cost roughly the same everywhere. The calculator's US pro range is $1–$6/sq ft; the $3.00 figure above is the national midpoint for exterior work.

  • Season: peak summer (June–August) pricing runs ~5–15% above shoulder season, and the best crews book out 3–6 weeks ahead.
  • Color change: dark-to-light (or light-to-dark) usually needs a primer coat plus full two-coat coverage — budget an extra 1–2 gallons and a day of labor.
  • Height and complexity: gables, dormers, and multi-level rooflines slow production even on "single-story" homes; each adds a few hundred dollars in labor.
  • Paint grade: contractor-grade (~$35/gal) vs. premium (~$65–$80/gal) changes a 14-gallon job by $400–$600 — but premium buys 3–5 extra years before the next repaint, making it the cheapest paint per year.

Best time of year to book exterior painting

Exterior paint is chemistry: most acrylics need 50–90°F and 24–48 dry hours to cure properly. Paint applied too cold, too hot, or ahead of rain can fail in a single season — which is why timing is a cost decision, not just a scheduling one.

  • Sweet spot: April–May and September–October. Mild temperatures, lower humidity, and crews with open calendars. Shoulder-season bids often come in 5–15% below peak summer.
  • Summer works, but costs more. June–August is peak demand: book 3–6 weeks out, expect the highest quotes of the year, and insist on early-morning starts so paint isn't applied to sun-baked 140°F siding.
  • Winter is mostly off the table in the northern two-thirds of the US. In the Sun Belt (Florida, Arizona, Southern California, Texas), winter is actually prime season — mild, dry, and discounted.
  • Fall has a hidden advantage: paint cures fully before winter's freeze-thaw cycles, and contractors are hungry to fill calendars before the holidays.

Whatever the season, never let a crew paint in direct hot sun, below 50°F, or when rain is due within 24 hours — the can's temperature specs are real, and failed paint is the most expensive paint.

How to save without cutting corners

  • Get three written, line-item quotes. The spread tells you the market; the line items tell you who's cutting prep. This one habit saves more than any coupon.
  • Book the shoulder season. Same crew, same paint, 5–15% less in April or October than in July.
  • Do the grunt work yourself: trimming bushes back from siding, moving patio furniture, and even renting a power washer for ~$75/day can erase a $300–$600 prep line item.
  • Keep the color family. A similar-color repaint skips the primer coat and sometimes gets away with one finish coat on sound siding — saving 1–2 gallons and a day of labor.
  • Don't downgrade the paint to save money. The $400–$600 saved on contractor-grade paint buys you a repaint 3–5 years sooner — at $4,000–$8,000 a pop. Premium paint is the cheapest line item per year of service.
  • Wash the siding yearly. A $150 maintenance wash every year or two extends the repaint cycle by 2–3 years by removing the mildew and grime that break paint down.

Red flags in exterior painting quotes

  • No written scope. "Paint house, $3,900" with no mention of prep, coats, or paint brand is a change-order waiting to happen.
  • No prep line item. If washing, scraping, and caulking aren't listed, they aren't happening — and the paint will fail early.
  • Deposit over one-third upfront. 10–25% to schedule is normal; half or more before work starts is not.
  • No license or insurance proof. Ask for both; a legitimate contractor produces them without blinking.
  • Weather-blind scheduling. A crew willing to paint at 45°F or with rain six hours out doesn't understand the product — walk away.
  • Cash-only, no contract. No paper trail means no warranty, no recourse, and usually no insurance.
  • "Leftover paint from another job" discounts. You don't know the color match, the age, or whether it was stored properly. Real savings come from scope and season, not mystery paint.

The bundling rule

Bundle add-ons with the wall job A standalone "just paint my ceilings" job carries full setup, masking, and cleanup costs — often a half-day minimum. Added to an in-progress wall repaint, the same ceiling is mostly marginal labor. Contractors routinely discount bundled trim/ceiling work 20–40% versus standalone quotes. Always ask: "what's the add-on price if you do it while you're here?"

DIY notes that actually matter

  • Ceilings: use dedicated ceiling paint (flat, low-spatter, ~$30–40/gal). A 12×15 ceiling needs one gallon. Roll away from the window light source to hide lap marks, and keep a wet edge — ceilings punish stop-and-start.
  • Doors: remove them and paint flat on sawhorses if you can; the finish difference vs. in-place brushing is dramatic. Budget a full day per batch for dry time between sides.
  • Trim: semi-gloss or satin enamel, and don't skip the light sanding between coats — it's the difference between "painted trim" and "factory trim."
  • Order of operations: ceilings first, then walls, then trim. Pros do it this way because drips only fall downward.

Trim is the classic DIY trap: it's genuinely easy, but a whole house of baseboards, casings, and crown can eat two full weekends. Pros handle it differently — they mask everything and spray, which is why a crew finishes in a day what takes you a fortnight. If you DIY the trim, invest in a quality angled sash brush; the $18 brush genuinely outperforms the $6 one, and you'll be holding it for hours.

Ceilings are the opposite trap: they look easy from the floor and punish you immediately. Rolling overhead with a wet edge, without stopping, across 400 sq ft is a skill — lap marks and roller stipple are the two most common DIY ceiling failures. If you're going to hire out one thing on this page, make it the ceilings. For exterior trim and siding, the access and prep math changes — see the exterior painting costs above.

How to measure a room in 2 minutes

  1. Measure the perimeter. Walk the room with a tape: length and width of each wall, or just the room's length × width — the calculator does 2×(L+W)×height for you.
  2. Use 8 ft unless you know better. Standard US ceilings are 8 ft; 9 ft in many newer homes. Vaulted ceilings need the average height.
  3. Count doors and windows. The calculator subtracts standard openings (21 sq ft per door, 15 per window) — close enough for estimating; pros measure exactly.
  4. Round paint up, never down. Running out mid-wall means a visible lap line and a second trip. The calculator's 10% waste allowance + round-up handles this.

Three ways people get the math wrong

  • Using floor square footage. A 12×15 room is 180 sq ft of floor but ~400 sq ft of wall. Quotes and calculators that don't specify which "square foot" they mean aren't comparable.
  • One coat fantasies. "One-coat coverage" is a marketing claim tested under lab conditions. Budget two coats; celebrate if one suffices.
  • Comparing price per gallon. A $74 gallon covering 425 sq ft can be cheaper per finished square foot than a $44 gallon covering 350. See our brand comparison for the real metric.

Paint terms worth knowing before you buy

A little vocabulary goes a long way at the paint counter. Sheen is the finish's gloss level — flat hides wall flaws but scuffs easily, eggshell and satin suit living areas, and semi-gloss belongs on trim and doors where durability matters. Cutting in means brush-painting edges, corners, and trim lines before rolling the big areas — it's the skill that separates pro-looking results from amateur ones. Roller nap is the cover's thickness: 3/8-inch for smooth walls, 1/2-inch for lightly textured surfaces. Low-VOC and zero-VOC paints emit fewer fumes, worth the small premium for bedrooms and nurseries. An airless sprayer is the pro tool for exteriors and whole-house repaints — fast, but it needs masking everything you don't want painted. And an accent wall — one bold wall in an otherwise neutral room — is the cheapest way to make a $150 paint job look like a $1,500 design choice (October 2026 planning estimates).

What paint returns at resale

Interior paint is one of the few projects that can return its cost at resale. Angi puts interior-paint ROI at around 107% — but that figure is an editorial estimate, not realtor-survey data, so treat it as directional, not a promise. Applied to our worked example: a $1,038 pro paint job × 107% ≈ $1,111 in resale value. The return depends on the market, the home's condition, and the color — neutral, well-executed paint helps; bold or sloppy paint does not. Source: Angi, "How Much Does Interior Paint Increase Home Value?"

Paint cost FAQs

How many gallons of paint do I need for a 12×12 room?

About 384 sq ft of wall after subtracting a door and window. At 400 sq ft per gallon and 2 coats, that's ~1.9 gallons — buy 2, or 3 with a waste allowance. Punch your exact dimensions into the calculator above.

Do I need primer before painting?

Yes for new drywall, bare wood, or dramatic color changes (dark to light). Usually no when repainting a similar color over sound paint — especially with a paint-and-primer-in-one. The calculator auto-selects primer based on the condition you pick.

How much does it cost to hire a painter vs. DIY?

Pros charge $1–$6 per sq ft of paintable area in 2026 (or $25–$75/hour); labor is typically 70–85% of a pro job's total. DIY for a 12×15 room runs roughly $150–$300 in paint and supplies. Toggle "Hire a pro" in the calculator to see both.

Is expensive paint worth it?

Often, measured correctly. Premium paints cover more square feet per gallon and hide in fewer coats, so cost per finished square foot can nearly match mid-range paint. Our Behr vs. Sherwin-Williams vs. Benjamin Moore comparison does this math with September 2026 shelf prices.

How many square feet does a gallon of paint cover on different surfaces?

Coverage drops as the surface gets rougher. Plan on about 400 sq ft per gallon on smooth drywall, 300–350 on textured walls, 250–300 on wood siding, and just 150–200 on brick or stucco — which is why exterior masonry jobs need far more paint than the wall area suggests. Premium paints hold the high end of each range; always add ~10% for waste. (October 2026 manufacturer specs.)

How much area does a gallon of primer cover?

Most primers cover 200–300 sq ft per gallon — noticeably less than topcoat paint, because primer goes on thicker and soaks into porous surfaces. New drywall, bare wood, and patched areas drink primer fastest, so budget the low end (200 sq ft) for those. One gallon typically primes the walls of a 12×12 room. Prices checked October 2026.

How much does it cost to paint a 12×12 bedroom?

DIY: roughly $140 (2 gallons of mid-range paint plus supplies). Pro: roughly $700–$1,000 at typical $2.50/sq ft labor — the US range spans $1–$6/sq ft by market. Run your exact room through the calculator above.

Why do painter quotes vary so much?

Labor rates vary $1–$6 per sq ft by market, prep assumptions differ enormously between bidders, and quotes use different bases (paintable sq ft vs. floor sq ft vs. per room). Normalize every quote to cost per paintable square foot before comparing.

Should I buy the paint or let the painter supply it?

Painters often get 10–30% contractor discounts, so their supply price can beat retail — but get the exact product spec (brand, line, sheen) in writing so you're not unknowingly downgraded to a cheaper line.

Does my region really change the price that much?

Yes — labor is 70–85% of the job, and 2026 guides put Southern labor lowest and Northeast/West Coast labor highest. On a 10×12 bedroom, our regional table runs from ~$844 in the South to ~$1,071 in the Northeast at the same paint spec. Get quotes from local painters and normalize each to cost per paintable square foot before comparing.

How long does it take to paint a bedroom?

A pro crew takes about 4–5 hours for a standard bedroom (Digital Estimating 2026), plus 1–2 hours if trim and baseboards are included. DIY takes longer once drying time between coats is counted — plan a full weekend for a bedroom, not an evening.

How much does it cost to paint the exterior of a 2,000 sq ft house?

DIY: roughly $850 in paint and supplies. Pro: roughly $6,500–$8,500 for a two-story home, depending on siding condition and market — the second story alone adds 25–50%. Get your exact figure with the calculator above set to Exterior.

Why is exterior painting so much more expensive than interior?

More surface area, far more prep (washing, scraping, caulking, repairs), access equipment for upper levels, and weather-rated paint at 15–25% more per gallon. On older homes, prep can be half the total labor.

How often should a house exterior be repainted?

Wood siding every 5–7 years, stucco 5–6, fiber cement 10–15, painted brick 15–20. Harsh sun, coastal air, and dark colors shorten the cycle.

How many gallons of exterior paint do I need?

Measure your siding square footage, divide by the paint's coverage (about 350 sq ft/gal for most exterior acrylics), multiply by 2 coats, add 10% for waste, and round up to whole gallons. A 2,000 sq ft two-story home with ~2,200 sq ft of siding needs roughly 14 gallons. Use the calculator above to do it precisely.

Can I paint vinyl or aluminum siding?

Yes, with the right system: clean thoroughly, apply a bonding primer rated for the siding, and use a vinyl-safe color no darker than the original — dark colors absorb heat and can warp vinyl. Check your siding warranty first; some manufacturers void it if painted. Expect an 8–10 year repaint cycle.

How long does an exterior paint job take?

A professional crew typically needs 3–5 days for a single-story home and 5–10 days for a two-story, including prep — weather delays are common and legitimate, since paint can't go on in rain or below 50°F. DIY on a single-story ranch is usually two to three full weekends for one careful person.

Is it cheaper to paint a house exterior in the fall?

Often yes. Fall (September–October) is shoulder season: ideal 50–90°F curing temperatures, drier air, and contractors with open calendars — bids frequently run 5–15% below peak summer. Spring (April–May) offers the same advantages. Even off-peak, book 3–6 weeks ahead; good crews stay busy.

How much does it cost to paint a ceiling?

$1–$2 per sq ft professionally — a 12×15 living room ceiling runs $180–$360. DIY: about $40–$60 for a gallon of ceiling paint and a roller cover.

How much to paint interior doors?

$75–$150 per door pro, both sides. DIY materials are trivial (a quart of enamel covers several doors) — you're paying for detailed brushwork and the drying logistics.

Is it cheaper to do trim at the same time as walls?

Almost always. Bundled add-ons skip duplicate setup/cleanup charges, and contractors commonly discount them 20–40% versus standalone jobs.

How much does it cost to paint a popcorn ceiling?

Painting over it costs the standard $1–$2/sq ft, but pros will warn you: paint makes future removal much harder. Removing popcorn first adds roughly $1–$2/sq ft. If the house predates 1978, test for asbestos before disturbing the texture — that's a lab test, not a guess.

Do painters charge extra for tall ceilings?

Yes — typically 25–40% more on ceiling line items for 10–12 ft heights (scaffolding, slower work). Vaulted or cathedral ceilings are usually quoted as a flat per-job price rather than per square foot.

Should ceilings be the same color as the walls?

Standard white flat is the safe default and costs the same either way. Same-color ceilings are a current designer trend that makes rooms feel larger — cost-neutral for materials, sometimes slightly cheaper on labor since there's less color-change cut-in. Dark ceilings are dramatic but show every drywall flaw.

How long does it take pros to paint a whole house of trim?

For a ~2,000 sq ft home: 2–3 days for a crew, usually sprayed. DIY with a brush: expect 2–4 weekends. The time gap is why whole-house trim is one of the strongest hire-a-pro candidates in interior painting.

Can I paint just the trim and skip the walls?

You can, but expect top-of-range pricing: standalone trim-only jobs typically land at $3–$5 per linear foot plus a half-day minimum ($250–$400), since the crew still masks and sets up the whole room. If the walls will need paint within a year or two, waiting and bundling is usually the cheaper move.
