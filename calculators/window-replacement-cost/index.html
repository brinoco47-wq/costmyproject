What this calculator estimates

This tool prices a window replacement job from the decisions that actually move the number: how many windows you're replacing, the window type (double-hung, casement, sliding, picture, bay/bow), the frame material (vinyl, wood, fiberglass, aluminum-clad wood), the installation method (insert/pocket vs. full-frame tear-out), whether you want triple-pane energy glass, and your region's labor rates. Every result shows the line-by-line math and a ±25% planning range — because this is a budgeting estimate, not a quote, and real bids move with rot repair, lead paint remediation, and local labor markets.

How the math works

  • Window unit: base price by type — double-hung $350, casement $400, sliding $350, picture $450, bay/bow $2,200 — multiplied by the frame material factor: vinyl 1.0×, aluminum-clad wood 1.25×, fiberglass 1.45×, wood 1.6×.
  • Energy upgrade: triple-pane ENERGY STAR glass adds $140 per window flat.
  • Installation labor: per window — insert (pocket) $200, full-frame tear-out $450 — multiplied by your region rate (0.85–1.2×) and your ZIP adjustment if entered.
  • Permits and disposal: $50 per window for haul-away; full-frame jobs add a $250 permit flat fee (most jurisdictions require one when the opening changes).
  • Rot repair: optional $150 per window carpentry allowance for rotted sills or jambs found on inspection.

Every figure is an October 2026 US planning estimate. Real quotes vary — that's why results show a ±25% planning range. This is a budgeting estimate, not a quote.

Insert vs. full-frame: the $250-per-window question

Insert (pocket) installation fits a new window inside your existing frame — the trim, casing and siding stay untouched. Labor runs ~$150–$250 per window and the job is fast (a crew does 10–15 inserts a day). It's the right choice when the existing frames are square, solid, and rot-free — which covers most 20–40-year-old homes.

Full-frame (tear-out) installation removes everything down to the rough opening — frame, trim, sometimes siding — and installs a completely new window with fresh flashing. Labor runs ~$350–$500 per window and it's the only correct answer when there's rot, water damage, or you want to change the window's size or style. It also lets the installer fix the water management the original builder got wrong.

The honest contractor test: if a salesperson pushes full-frame on every window without checking the frames for rot, get a second quote. And if a quote is insert-priced but the installer finds rot mid-job, expect a change order — the $150/window rot allowance in this calculator exists for exactly that surprise.

Labor: why quotes vary so much

Window units are nationally priced — a vinyl double-hung costs the dealer about the same in Ohio and Oregon. Installation labor is not. Our calculator scales labor by region (rural 0.85× through West Coast 1.2×) and, if you enter a ZIP, by your metro's actual BLS construction wages. On the $6,000 worked example above, labor is $2,000 in the South — the same job prices at roughly $1,700 in a rural market and $2,400 on the West Coast. High-cost metros (Bay Area, NYC, Boston) can run 30–40% above national labor. When a bid's labor line looks wildly off these bands, ask what it includes — disposal, interior trim repair, and lead-safe practices (required by EPA RRP rules in pre-1978 homes) are the usual hidden labor lines. The ENERGY STAR windows program and the Efficient Windows Collaborative both publish buyer guidance worth reading before you sign.

Energy efficiency that actually pays

Every replacement window sold today is double-pane with low-E coating and argon fill as standard — a huge step up from the single-pane aluminum windows in older homes. ENERGY STAR estimates that replacing single-pane windows with ENERGY STAR models saves roughly $100–$580 a year on energy bills, depending on climate and house size. Triple-pane (an extra ~$140/window in this calculator) earns its keep in cold climates — northern zones, where heating dominates — and is overkill in mild ones.

The numbers that matter on the sticker are U-factor (heat loss; lower is better, look for ≤0.30 in cold climates) and SHGC (solar heat gain; lower blocks more sun — good in the South, bad in the North where you want winter sun). Don't buy on brand alone: a $600 vinyl window with U-0.28 outperforms a $1,200 wood window with U-0.32 on energy. The frame material is about looks and longevity; the glass package is about the utility bill.

Window terms worth knowing

Contractor quotes read like a different language. The terms that actually affect your price:

  • Sash — the moving frame that holds the glass. Double-hung windows have two operable sashes; single-hung has one.
  • Jamb — the side frame of the window opening. Rot here is what forces full-frame replacement.
  • Sill — the bottom horizontal piece. The most rot-prone part of any wood window.
  • U-factor — the rate of heat loss through the whole window. Lower is better; ≤0.30 is the cold-climate benchmark.
  • SHGC (solar heat gain coefficient) — how much sun heat passes through. 0–1 scale; lower means less heat — ideal in hot climates.
  • Low-E (low emissivity) — a microscopically thin metallic coating on the glass that reflects heat. Standard on quality windows since the 2000s.
  • Argon fill — inert gas between the panes, denser than air, that slows heat transfer. Standard in double-pane; krypton is the pricier upgrade.
  • Insert / pocket replacement — new window fitted inside the existing frame. Cheapest install method.
  • Full-frame / tear-out — complete removal to the rough opening. Required for rot, resizing, or style changes.
  • Egress — a window large enough for emergency escape, required by code in bedrooms. Egress windows cost more because of minimum size rules.
  • Muntins / grilles — the dividers that make one pane look like many. Between-glass grilles are maintenance-free; true divided lites cost 2–3× more.
  • NFRC label — the National Fenestration Rating Council sticker with certified U-factor and SHGC. Compare labels, not brochures.

How to save $2,000 on window replacement

  • Do all the windows at once. Full-house jobs (10+) typically earn 10–20% volume discounts on both units and labor. Two half-jobs cost more than one whole job.
  • Choose insert installation where the frames are sound. ~$200/window vs. ~$450 for full-frame — on 10 windows that's $2,500 saved with zero visible difference.
  • Pick vinyl for the back of the house. Nobody sees the rear elevation up close. Splurge on wood or fiberglass for the street-facing windows, vinyl everywhere else.
  • Skip triple-pane in mild climates. The $140/window upgrade pays back in Minnesota, not in San Diego.
  • Get three quotes, and make them itemized. Per-window unit price, per-window labor, disposal, permits. A lump-sum quote hides the padding.
  • Time it for late fall or winter. Installers discount in the slow season, and modern installs take under an hour per window — your house isn't open to the cold all day.

Manufacturer pricing context helps at the negotiating table: Andersen and Pella publish product lines and performance data, and the Journal of Light Construction tracks installed-cost data from working contractors. The National Association of Home Builders also publishes construction cost surveys that keep our labor bands honest.

Window replacement cost FAQs

How much does window replacement cost per window in 2026?

Most US homeowners pay $450–$1,000 per window installed. A standard vinyl double-hung runs $450–$800, wood or fiberglass $800–$1,500, and a bay or bow window $2,500–$5,000. Insert (pocket) installation is cheaper than full-frame tear-out by roughly $250 per window. Use the calculator above for your window count, type and material.

Is it cheaper to replace all windows at once?

Yes — contractors typically discount 10–20% on full-house jobs (10+ windows) because the crew mobilizes once and material orders get volume pricing. Replacing 10 vinyl double-hungs at once usually lands at $4,500–$7,000; doing the same 10 in two batches of five can cost 15% more in total.

What is the cheapest window replacement option?

A vinyl double-hung with insert (pocket) installation — roughly $450–$650 per window installed. Vinyl is the cheapest frame material, double-hung is the simplest operable type, and insert installation reuses the existing frame so labor stays low.

How much does it cost to replace 10 windows?

About $4,500–$8,000 for 10 vinyl double-hungs with insert installation, $8,000–$15,000 for wood or fiberglass frames, and $3,000–$4,000 more if every window needs full-frame tear-out. Punch your exact count, type and material into the calculator for a line-by-line number.

Insert vs. full-frame: which installation is better?

Insert (pocket) installation — a new window fitted inside the existing frame — is cheaper (~$200/window labor) and right for most replacements when the frame is square and rot-free. Full-frame tear-out (~$450/window) replaces everything down to the studs and is the fix for rotted frames, water damage, or changing the window size or style.

Do energy-efficient windows pay for themselves?

Eventually, but slowly on energy alone: ENERGY STAR estimates that replacing single-pane windows with ENERGY STAR models saves roughly $100–$580 a year on energy bills. The real payback is comfort (no more drafts), noise reduction, and resale value — mid-range window jobs recoup roughly 65–70% at resale.

How long do replacement windows last?

Vinyl windows: 20–30 years. Fiberglass: 30–40 years. Wood: 30+ years with maintenance (15–20 without). Aluminum-clad wood: 30+ years. Failed seals (fog between the panes) are the usual end of life for insulated glass — most quality windows carry 20-year glass warranties.

When should I replace vs. repair my windows?

Repair when the issue is cosmetic or minor: a broken latch, worn weatherstripping, or a single failed sash balance. Replace when you see fogged glass between panes (failed seal), rotted or soft frames, persistent drafts after weatherstripping, or single-pane glass in a cold climate — repairs on failed seals are temporary and cost nearly as much as a new insert.
