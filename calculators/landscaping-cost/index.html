What this calculator estimates

This tool prices a landscaping project from the decisions that actually move the number: the project area in square feet, the project type (brand-new installation, renovation of an existing yard, or a cleanup and mulch refresh), the features you include (sod, planting beds, trees, patio or pavers, retaining wall, irrigation, landscape lighting, mulch), how hard the site is to access, and your region's labor rates. Feature quantities scale with your yard size using typical coverage ratios — every result shows the line-by-line math and a ±25% planning range, because this is a budgeting estimate, not a quote, and real bids move with soil conditions, drainage surprises, plant selection, and local labor markets.

How the math works

  • Site preparation base: every project starts with prep — grading, soil amendment, debris removal, and layout. New installation $1.80/sq ft, renovation $1.20/sq ft (some existing material is salvaged), cleanup $0.60/sq ft (mostly labor: trimming, weeding, edging). This base covers the crew getting the site ready, not the visible features.
  • Features (installed prices): each ticked feature adds a line item with a quantity derived from your area using typical coverage ratios — sod at $1.75/sq ft over 60% of the area, planting beds at $10/sq ft over 25% of the area, trees at $475 each (one per 800 sq ft, minimum 2), mulch or rock at $1.10/sq ft over 30% of the area, patio/pavers at $18/sq ft over 15% of the area, retaining wall at $35/sq ft of wall face over 6% of the area, irrigation at $2.75/sq ft over the full area, and landscape lighting at $275 per fixture (one per 350 sq ft, minimum 6).
  • Site access multiplier: easy 1.0×, average 1.1×, difficult 1.25× on the whole project. A backyard with a 36-inch gate and no machine access can genuinely cost a quarter more — every yard of soil and every pallet of pavers arrives by wheelbarrow.
  • Labor region: rural 0.85×, South 1.0×, Midwest 0.95×, Northeast 1.15×, West coast 1.2×. If you enter a ZIP, the labor half of the estimate is rescaled with BLS wage data for your area — materials stay at national prices.
  • What's not included: landscape design fees ($500–$5,000 — see the guide), drainage systems beyond basic grading, tree removal, permits (rarely needed except for walls over 3–4 feet), and ongoing maintenance.

Every figure is an October 2026 US planning estimate. Real quotes vary — that's why results show a ±25% planning range. This is a budgeting estimate, not a quote.

Front yard vs. backyard

Front yards usually cost $5,000–$15,000: they're smaller, but they get the curb-appeal budget — entry beds, a walkway refresh, foundation plantings, and lighting. This is also where the ROI lives (see the guide). Backyards run $10,000–$30,000+: bigger area, and they carry the expensive features — patios, retaining walls, irrigation, drainage, and play or lounge zones. The most common budget mistake is splitting 50/50; most designers put 60–70% of the budget in the back where you actually live, unless you're selling soon.

Hardscaping vs. softscaping

Softscaping is everything alive or loose — sod, plants, trees, mulch, soil. It runs $1–$15 per square foot installed and it's what makes a yard look finished fastest. Hardscaping is everything built — patios, walls, walkways, fire pits, outdoor kitchens. It runs $10–$50+ per square foot and it's what makes projects expensive: a mostly-planted 5,000 sq ft yard might be $25,000 while the same yard with a big paver patio and two retaining walls is $60,000. The smart sequence for tight budgets is softscape first (the yard looks transformed), hardscape in phase two — unlike a kitchen, a yard tolerates phasing beautifully.

How to save $5,000 on landscaping

  • Seed instead of sod. Hydroseed or broadcast seed costs roughly a tenth of sod. You trade 6–8 weeks of patience for thousands of dollars.
  • Phase it. Do grading, drainage and the front yard in year one; the backyard patio in year two. Phasing also lets you learn what you actually use.
  • Buy small plants. A 1-gallon shrub at $15 becomes a 5-gallon shrub at $60 in two growing seasons. Small stock establishes faster and catches up.
  • Do the demo and cleanup yourself. Removing old sod, pulling dead shrubs, and spreading your own mulch are the easiest DIY savings in landscaping — often $2,000–$4,000 off the bid.
  • Fix drainage with grading, not drains. Regrading a swale ($500–$1,500) beats a French drain system ($2,000–$5,000) wherever the slope allows it.
  • Get three itemized bids. Landscaping bids vary more than almost any home trade — 40%+ spreads on the same scope are routine. Itemization is what lets you compare.

Cost context: the National Association of Landscape Professionals (NALP) publishes industry benchmarks and contractor standards, Angi's landscaping cost data tracks national price ranges from millions of real projects, and university extension services (like UGA Extension and Colorado State Extension) offer region-specific planting and water guidance without a sales pitch. Landscaping terms worth knowing

The vocabulary that keeps a bid honest:

  • Hardscaping — built elements: patios, walls, walkways. The expensive half of most projects.
  • Softscaping — living and loose elements: sod, plants, trees, mulch, soil.
  • Xeriscaping — landscaping designed to need minimal water: native plants, rock, drip irrigation. Big in the West and Southwest.
  • Grading — shaping the soil surface so water flows away from the house. The unglamorous work that prevents expensive problems.
  • Topsoil / amendment — the growing medium. Most new-construction lots need 2–4 inches of decent topsoil added; clay subsoil grows weeds, not lawns.
  • Sod vs. seed — sod is mature grass rolled out for an instant lawn ($1–$2/sq ft); seed is pennies per foot but needs weeks and careful watering.
  • Paver — manufactured concrete units for patios and walkways, $10–$25/sq ft installed. Natural stone costs more and varies more.
  • Retaining wall — a wall holding back soil on a slope. Over 3–4 feet tall it usually needs engineering and a permit.
  • French drain — a gravel-filled trench with perforated pipe that carries subsurface water away. $2,000–$5,000 for a typical run.
  • Drip irrigation — low-flow watering at the plant base. Uses far less water than spray heads; ideal for beds and xeriscapes.
  • Curb appeal — how the property presents from the street. The front yard's entire economic job.
  • Edging — the border between lawn and beds (steel, stone, or plastic). Cheap, and it makes everything look intentional.

Landscaping cost FAQs

How much does landscaping cost in 2026?

Landscaping costs $4–$12 per square foot for a new installation in 2026, or roughly $15,000–$40,000 for a full front-and-back yard on a typical suburban lot. A simple refresh runs $3,000–$8,000, while a design with patio, retaining walls and irrigation can pass $50,000. Use the calculator above for your yard size and features.

How much does landscaping cost per square foot?

Expect $4–$12 per square foot installed for new landscaping in 2026, $3–$8 per square foot for a renovation, and $0.50–$2 per square foot for a cleanup and mulch refresh. Hardscaping pushes the per-foot number up; simple sod and beds keep it down.

What is the cheapest way to landscape a yard?

The cheapest path is a cleanup plus mulch refresh ($0.50–$2 per square foot), DIY planting from nursery stock, and phasing the project over two or three seasons. Seed instead of sod (about a tenth of the cost), use native plants that need less water, and skip hardscaping until phase two.

How much does a front yard landscape cost vs. a backyard?

Front yards usually cost $5,000–$15,000 — they're smaller but get the curb-appeal budget (entry beds, walkway, lighting). Backyards run $10,000–$30,000+ because they're bigger and carry the expensive features: patios, retaining walls, irrigation and drainage.

Is hardscaping or softscaping more expensive?

Hardscaping is far more expensive: patios run $10–$25 per square foot installed and retaining walls $15–$50 per square foot of wall face, versus $1–$2 per square foot for sod and $5–$15 per square foot for planting beds. A project that is mostly hardscape can cost 2–3× a mostly-planted one at the same size.

How much does landscape design cost?

A professional landscape design costs $500–$5,000 in 2026 — roughly $300–$500 for a simple planting plan, $1,500–$3,000 for a full front-and-back design with hardscape details, and up to $5,000+ for large or complex sites. Many design-build firms credit some or all of the design fee if you hire them for the install.

Does landscaping increase home value?

Yes — studies consistently show quality landscaping returning 100–200% of its cost at resale through curb appeal, with maintained landscapes adding an estimated 5–12% to perceived home value. The payback is strongest on the front yard and weakest on ultra-high-end backyard builds that exceed neighborhood norms.

How much does yard maintenance cost per month?

Full-service yard maintenance costs $150–$400 per month in 2026 for a typical suburban lot (mowing, edging, bed upkeep, seasonal pruning). Mow-only service runs $50–$150 per month. Irrigation winterization, aeration and fertilization are usually billed separately, $75–$300 per visit.
