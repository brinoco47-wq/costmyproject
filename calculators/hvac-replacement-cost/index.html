What this calculator estimates

This tool prices a full HVAC replacement from the decisions that actually move the number: your home's square footage (which sets the system tonnage), the system type (central AC + gas furnace, air-source heat pump, ductless mini-split, or packaged unit), the efficiency tier (14, 16, or 18+ SEER2), whether your ductwork is reused, modified, or replaced, and your region's labor rates. Every result shows the line-by-line math and a ±25% planning range — because this is a budgeting estimate, not a quote, and real bids move with electrical upgrades, refrigerant line replacement, permits, and local labor markets.

How the math works

  • System size: tonnage = home square footage ÷ 550, rounded to the nearest half-ton (1 ton = 12,000 BTU/hr of cooling). A 2,000 sq ft home lands at ~3.5 tons. This is a planning rule of thumb — a real Manual J load calculation also weighs insulation, windows, and climate.
  • Equipment: base equipment cost at 3 tons — central AC + furnace $5,200, heat pump $6,000, packaged unit $5,400 — scaled by (your tons ÷ 3)0.85, because bigger systems cost more but not linearly. Ductless mini-splits are priced per zone instead: zones = square footage ÷ 600 (2–8 zones), $3,400 per installed zone.
  • Efficiency multiplier: 14 SEER2 = 1.0×, 16 SEER2 = 1.18×, 18+ SEER2 = 1.38× on equipment (mini-splits: 1.0× / 1.15× / 1.30×). Higher SEER2 mostly buys inverter-driven variable-speed compressors.
  • Installation labor: flat by system type — central $2,800, heat pump $3,000, packaged $2,600 (mini-split labor is bundled in the per-zone price) — multiplied by your region rate (0.85–1.2×) and your ZIP adjustment if entered.
  • Ductwork: reuse $0, modify/seal $0.80 per sq ft, new ducts $2.75 per sq ft. Ignored for ductless mini-splits.
  • Electrical + pad + permit: $600 flat for disconnect, whip, pad and minor electrical; $350 permit flat fee (most jurisdictions require one for a full changeout).

Every figure is an October 2026 US planning estimate. Real quotes vary — that's why results show a ±25% planning range. This is a budgeting estimate, not a quote.

SEER2 explained: what efficiency actually buys

SEER2 (Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio 2) is the DOE's current test procedure for cooling efficiency — seasonal cooling output divided by seasonal electricity input. Higher = less electricity for the same cooling. The federal minimum since 2023 is 14 SEER2 in most of the country (15.2 SEER2 in the Southeast and Southwest for split systems).

  • 14 SEER2 — single-stage compressor: full blast or off. Cheapest, loudest, least comfortable. Fine for mild climates and tight budgets.
  • 16 SEER2 — usually two-stage: runs low most of the time, kicks to high on brutal days. ~12–15% lower cooling bills than 14 SEER2, noticeably quieter and better at dehumidifying.
  • 18+ SEER2 — inverter-driven variable speed: modulates continuously like a dimmer switch. 20–30% lower cooling bills, near-silent, best humidity control. The premium is real (~30–40% over 14 SEER2).

The payback math: on the 2,000 sq ft worked example, moving 14 → 16 SEER2 adds ~$1,060 in equipment and saves roughly $120–$180/year in a hot climate — a 6–9 year payback. Moving 14 → 18+ adds ~$2,250 and saves ~$200–$300/year — 8–11 years. In a mild climate, halve the savings and double the payback. Efficiency also has a comfort dividend the math misses: variable-speed systems hold temperature within a degree and wring out humidity that single-stage units leave behind.

Ductwork: the $0–$8,000 swing

Ducts are the forgotten half of the system. The DOE estimates the average home loses 20–30% of conditioned air to duct leaks, poor connections, and ducts routed through unconditioned attics. Bolting a $12,000 high-efficiency system onto leaky 30-year-old ducts is like putting racing tires on a car with a cracked frame.

  • Reuse ($0) — right when ducts are properly sized, sealed, and insulated, and the new system's airflow requirements match. Have them pressure-tested; a duct-blaster test runs $200–$400 and ends all guessing.
  • Modify / seal ($0.80/sq ft, ~$1,000–$3,000) — Aeroseal or manual mastic sealing of accessible ducts, plus adding a return or two. The best dollars-per-BTU you'll spend if your ducts leak.
  • New ducts ($2.75/sq ft, ~$3,000–$8,000) — required when ducts are undersized for the new system, crushed, moldy, or routed so badly that sealing can't save them. Also the moment to move ducts inside conditioned space if you're renovating anyway.

Mini-splits sidestep the whole question — no ducts, no duct losses, which is part of why their real-world efficiency often beats the sticker numbers.

Rebates and tax credits (2026)

HVAC is one of the most subsidized purchases a homeowner can make. Stack what's available before you sign:

  • Federal 25C credit — ENDED for 2026 installs. The energy-efficient home improvement credit (up to $2,000/year on heat pumps, up to $1,200/year on other efficient equipment) applied only to property placed in service before December 31, 2025, when the One Big Beautiful Bill Act terminated it. A 2026 HVAC install gets no federal credit under 25C. If you installed qualifying equipment in 2025, you can still claim it on that return — keep your AHRI certificate.
  • Utility rebates — $300–$2,000. Most major utilities rebate high-SEER2 systems, smart thermostats, and duct sealing. Check the ENERGY STAR rebate finder and your utility's site before buying — some rebates require pre-approval.
  • State programs — varies widely. States with aggressive electrification goals (California, New York, Massachusetts, Colorado) layer thousands more on heat pumps, sometimes income-qualified up to full cost.
  • Manufacturer promotions — $500–$1,500. Spring and fall promos from Carrier, Trane, Lennox and Daikin are routine; ask your contractor what's running.

A heat pump that quoted at $13,000 in 2025 could net under $10,000 after the (then-available) $2,000 federal credit and a $1,000 utility rebate. For 2026 installs, the federal credit is gone — so do the after-rebate math on utility and state incentives only, not the sticker math, and get quotes both ways.

How to save $3,000 on HVAC replacement

  • Get three Manual J–based quotes. Any contractor who sizes by square footage alone is guessing with your money. The load calculation is the difference between a system that sips and a system that struggles.
  • Buy in the shoulder seasons. Late fall and early spring are slow for HVAC companies — discounts of 5–15% are common, and install slots are immediate instead of 3-week waits.
  • Seal ducts before upsizing equipment. A $1,500 duct-sealing job can let you buy a half-ton smaller system — saving $800–$1,500 on equipment while cutting bills permanently.
  • Don't oversize. Bigger is not better: an oversized AC short-cycles, leaving humidity high and wearing components faster. The right size runs longer, quieter, cheaper.
  • Claim every incentive that still exists. The federal 25C credit ended for 2026 installs, but utility and state rebates routinely shave $500–$2,000 off heat pumps. Contractors won't always volunteer this — ask.
  • Bundle the thermostat. A $250 smart thermostat the installer configures during commissioning saves 8–10% on heating/cooling annually — and costs triple as a separate service call later.

Equipment pricing context: ENERGY STAR's heating and cooling pages list certified models by efficiency, the DOE Energy Saver guides explain sizing and maintenance without a sales pitch, AHRI's directory lets you verify any system's certified ratings, and ACCA (the contractors' own standards body) publishes the Manual J methodology your contractor should be following. HVAC terms worth knowing

The vocabulary that keeps a quote honest:

  • SEER2 — Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio (current DOE test). Cooling efficiency; higher is better. 14 is the 2026 minimum in most regions.
  • HSPF2 — Heating Seasonal Performance Factor 2. The heating-side twin of SEER2 for heat pumps; higher is better.
  • AFUE — Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency. Furnace efficiency as a percentage; 80% is builder-grade, 95%+ is high-efficiency condensing.
  • Tonnage — cooling capacity; 1 ton = 12,000 BTU/hr. A 3.5-ton system moves 42,000 BTU/hr.
  • Manual J — ACCA's room-by-room heating/cooling load calculation. The only legitimate way to size a system.
  • Single-stage / two-stage / variable-speed — compressor operation: on/off, low/high, or continuously modulating. Comfort and efficiency rise in that order.
  • Refrigerant (R-410A / R-454B) — the heat-transfer fluid. R-410A is being phased down; new systems increasingly use lower-GWP R-454B. Never mix refrigerants.
  • Evaporator coil / condenser — the indoor and outdoor halves of a split system. Replace both together or efficiency and warranty suffer.
  • Heat exchanger — the furnace component that keeps combustion gases out of your air. A cracked one means replace the furnace, full stop.
  • Duct blaster / Aeroseal — a pressure test measuring duct leakage, and an aerosol sealant that plugs leaks from inside. The two best duct diagnostics and fixes.
  • Short-cycling — an oversized system turning on and off rapidly. Wastes energy, skips dehumidification, shortens equipment life.
  • AHRI certificate — the matched-system efficiency rating. You need it for tax credits and rebate paperwork — get it from your contractor.

HVAC replacement cost FAQs

How much does it cost to replace an HVAC system in 2026?

A full HVAC replacement costs $7,500–$18,000 in 2026 for most US homes. A central AC + gas furnace runs $7,500–$15,000, an air-source heat pump $8,000–$16,000, a ductless mini-split $6,000–$14,000 depending on zones, and a packaged unit $7,000–$12,000. New ductwork adds $3,000–$8,000 on top. Use the calculator above for your home size, system type and efficiency.

How much does a new AC unit cost vs. a full HVAC system?

Replacing just the outdoor AC condenser runs $3,500–$7,500 installed, while a full system (condenser + indoor coil or air handler + furnace) runs $7,500–$15,000. Replacing only half the system is cheaper upfront but mismatched old and new components cut efficiency and usually void the manufacturer's warranty — most pros recommend replacing indoor and outdoor units together.

What size HVAC system do I need for my house?

As a rough rule, homes need about 1 ton of cooling per 500–600 square feet — so a 2,000 sq ft home needs roughly 3.5 tons. But square footage alone is unreliable: insulation, window area, ceiling height and climate change the answer. The correct method is a Manual J load calculation, which a good contractor performs before quoting. Never let anyone size your system by square footage alone.

Is a heat pump cheaper than a furnace and AC?

Upfront, a cold-climate heat pump ($8,000–$16,000 installed) costs about the same or slightly more than a mid-efficiency furnace + AC combo ($7,500–$15,000). Over time the heat pump usually wins on operating cost — it moves heat instead of making it, delivering 2–4× the energy per dollar in moderate climates. Note: the federal 25C credit (up to $2,000 on heat pumps) ended for installs after December 31, 2025 — a 2026 install gets no federal credit, though state and utility rebates still apply. In very cold climates, keep a gas furnace as backup or buy a cold-climate rated model.

What does SEER2 mean, and is higher SEER2 worth it?

SEER2 (Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio 2) measures cooling efficiency under the current DOE test procedure — higher means less electricity for the same cooling. The 2026 federal minimum is 14 SEER2 in most of the US. Jumping from 14 to 16 SEER2 adds roughly 15–20% to equipment cost and cuts cooling bills about 12–15%; 18+ SEER2 (inverter/variable-speed) adds 30–40% and cuts bills 20–30%. Higher SEER2 pays back fastest where summers are long and electricity is expensive.

How much does new ductwork add to HVAC replacement?

New ductwork adds $3,000–$8,000 for a typical home — roughly $2.50–$3.50 per square foot of living space. Modifying or sealing existing ducts runs $1,000–$3,000. Reusing ducts in good condition costs nothing extra, but leaky or undersized ducts can waste 20–30% of your new system's output, so have them pressure-tested before you decide.

Are there tax credits for HVAC replacement in 2026?

No federal credit for 2026 installs — the 25C energy-efficient home improvement credit (up to $2,000 per year on qualifying heat pumps, up to $1,200 per year on other efficient equipment) applied only to property placed in service before December 31, 2025, when it was terminated. State and utility rebates still apply, sometimes $500–$2,000 more. If you installed qualifying equipment in 2025 and have not filed yet, you can still claim it on that return — keep your AHRI certificate and receipts.

Repair vs. replace: when is replacement the right call?

Replace when the system is 12–15+ years old and facing a major repair (compressor, heat exchanger, evaporator coil), when repairs in the last two years exceed about a third of replacement cost, or when it uses R-22 refrigerant (phased out — recharges are brutally expensive). Repair when it's under 10 years old, the fix is minor (capacitor, contactor, thermostat), and efficiency is still reasonable.
