✓ Included in every estimate

  • Installation labor and permitting
  • State incentives you enter (the federal §25D credit ended Dec 31, 2025 — see the tax-credit FAQ below)
  • Production estimate with an 80% real-world derate (heat, dust, inverter losses)

✗ Not included

  • Battery storage (typically $9,000–$15,000 extra)
  • Main panel upgrades (often $1,500–$3,000 if needed)
  • Roof replacement or major roof repair before install
  • Tree removal or heavy shading mitigation

How to size a system in 3 steps

  1. Find your annual kWh. Check 12 months of electric bills (or one bill × 12). This — not your roof size — sets the system size.
  2. Divide by your sun hours. Annual kWh ÷ (365 × peak sun hours × 0.80) gives the kW you need. Your state solar office or PVWatts publishes local sun-hour figures.
  3. Sanity-check the roof. Each 400 W panel needs about 18–20 sq ft. A 21-panel system needs roughly 400 sq ft of usable, unshaded roof facing south, east, or west.

Three ways people get solar math wrong

  • Counting a federal credit that no longer exists. Quotes and calculators built before 2026 still show 30% off — for a 2026 homeowner install that discount is gone. If a quote's "net cost" subtracts a federal credit, ask which credit and whether you can actually claim it.
  • Sizing by roof, not by bill. A roof that fits 30 panels doesn't mean you need 30 panels — oversizing wastes money unless net metering pays full retail for exports.
  • Forgetting the derate. A 8 kW system does not produce 8 kW all day. The 0.80 factor in this calculator accounts for heat, dust, and inverter losses — quotes that skip it overpromise production by ~20%.
  • Comparing gross prices. Always compare net cost after any state incentives, and always compare cost per watt — not headline totals for different-sized systems.

Net metering: why your payback depends on it

Your payback years hinge on one policy as much as on sunshine: net metering — how your utility credits you for power your panels send to the grid. Under full retail net metering, every exported kilowatt-hour offsets a kilowatt-hour you buy later, so the calculator's simple payback math holds. Under newer rules in several states, exports earn only a wholesale or avoided-cost rate (sometimes 25–40% of retail), which stretches payback by years unless you shift usage into daylight hours or add a battery. Before signing a contract, ask your installer to model your bill under your utility's current export rate, not last year's, and check whether your state is scheduled to change its net metering rules — that single number can matter more than panel brand or a $0.10/W price difference.

Solar cost FAQs

How many solar panels do I need for my home?

It depends on your electricity use, not your roof size. A home using 900 kWh per month with 4.5 peak sun hours needs about an 8.2 kW system — roughly 21 panels at 400 watts each. Enter your bill above for your exact number.

How much do solar panels cost in 2026?

About $2.50–$3.00 per watt installed, so a typical 7–8 kW system runs $17,500–$24,000 — and in 2026 that is also the net cost for homeowner-owned systems, since the former 30% federal credit ended Dec 31, 2025. State and utility incentives can still reduce it.

Is there still a 30% federal solar tax credit in 2026?

No — not for homeowner-owned systems. The Section 25D residential clean energy credit ended for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025, under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (July 2025). A system installed in 2026 gets no federal credit, even if you signed or paid in 2025 — what counts is when installation finished. Leased and PPA systems may carry indirect value through the installer's separate commercial credit, and state and utility incentives still apply — check the DSIRE database for your state.

How long until solar panels pay for themselves?

Without the former federal credit, most US homes break even in roughly 10–16 years. High-rate states can pay back in 8–10 years; low-rate states take longer. The calculator above computes your payback from your actual bill and rate.

Do solar panels work on cloudy days or in winter?

Yes, at reduced output — roughly 10–25% of rated power under heavy cloud, and less in winter with shorter days. The peak-sun-hours figure is a yearly average, so annual estimates already include this.

Do I need a battery with solar panels?

Not necessarily. Without one, excess daytime power goes to the grid (net metering where available) and you draw from the grid at night. A battery adds $9,000–$15,000 and makes sense mainly for backup power or poor net-metering terms.
