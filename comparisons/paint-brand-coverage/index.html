What the real metric reveals

  • Benjamin Moore beats Sherwin-Williams on value despite the higher sticker price — Regal's superior coverage (up to 450 sq ft/gal) makes it ~7% cheaper per finished square foot than SuperPaint at list prices.
  • Behr is the clear value winner at ~$22 per 100 sq ft — roughly 40% cheaper per finished square foot than either premium brand.
  • The Sherwin-Williams sale changes everything. SW runs 30–40% off sales several times a year. At 40% off ($42/gal), SuperPaint's true cost drops to ~$22.40 per 100 sq ft — dead even with Behr. Never buy SuperPaint at full price if a sale is within a few weeks.
Our take, plainly For DIY repaints on a budget: Behr Premium Plus is genuinely hard to beat. For pros and perfectionists: buy whichever premium brand is on sale — at sale prices the value gap vanishes and you're choosing on hide, color accuracy, and store convenience. Brand loyalty at full retail price is the most expensive option on this page.

The pro painter angle: why contractors don't buy like you do

Two things change the math when a pro buys the paint. First, contractor pricing: painters with store accounts typically pay 15–30% below list, and Sherwin-Williams in particular is built around this — its list prices are high partly because its contractor prices are not. A painter paying $50/gal for SuperPaint has a true cost near Behr's retail, which is one reason SW dominates pro trucks.

Second, labor dwarfs materials. On a $4,000 interior job, the paint might be $400–$600 — 10–15% of the bill. A pro will happily spend $200 more on paint that covers in two guaranteed coats instead of three, because the third coat costs them $800 in labor. When your painter insists on a brand, it's usually about their economics (reliable coverage, accurate color matching, store support) rather than yours — and it's usually correct.

Contractor discount ranges are industry estimates. If you're supplying your own paint to save money, ask the painter which line they want — then buy exactly that during a sale.

FAQs

Is Benjamin Moore worth it over Behr?

Per finished square foot, Regal Select (~$34.82/100 sq ft) costs about 58% more than Behr Premium Plus (~$21.99). You're paying for better hide, richer color, and durability — worth it for showcase rooms, harder to justify for a rental refresh.

Is Sherwin-Williams SuperPaint better than Behr Premium Plus?

It's more durable with excellent hide — a contractor favorite for good reason. But at list price it's ~70% more per finished square foot than Behr. The smart move: buy SuperPaint only during 30–40% off sales, when the value gap disappears.

Which covers the most square feet per gallon?

Benjamin Moore Regal Select (400–450 sq ft/gal), then Behr Premium Plus (400), then SW SuperPaint (350–400) — all manufacturer specs. Real-world coverage runs lower on textured or porous surfaces.

Should I buy the premium tier or the mid-tier?

Match the tier to the room: budget for closets and low-visibility walls, mid-tier for living spaces, premium for kitchens, front doors, and anywhere you'll study the finish daily. Whole-house premium is a luxury; whole-house budget is usually a false economy — the repaint arrives years sooner.

Do I still need primer with "paint and primer in one"?

On previously painted walls in a similar color: usually not — that's what the products are formulated for. On bare drywall, dramatic color changes, or stain blocking: yes, use real primer regardless of the label. A $25 primer gallon under a $44 topcoat often outperforms a $74 topcoat struggling alone.

Why do painters insist on Sherwin-Williams or Benjamin Moore?

Mostly their economics, and it's usually sound: contractor pricing (15–30% off list), reliable two-coat coverage, accurate color matching, and store support. Since labor dwarfs materials on a pro job, a painter will spend more on paint that saves them a coat — that third coat costs them far more in labor than the paint upgrade costs you.

How much extra paint should I buy?

About 10% over the calculated gallons — for waste, cutting-in, and touch-ups. A leftover quart of the exact batch is worth more than its price when you need to touch up a scuff in two years; paint mixed later never matches perfectly.
