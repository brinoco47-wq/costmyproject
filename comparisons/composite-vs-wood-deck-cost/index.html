Composite vs. Wood Deck: 25-Year Head-to-Head

Advertisement

CostMyProject Research Team Prices checked October 2026

The most expensive deck argument on the internet, settled with arithmetic. A 400-sqft composite deck costs $5,200 more on day one — and finishes 25 years $11,500 cheaper than pressure-treated wood. Here's the year-by-year math.

Day one: 400-sqft deck, pro install, ground-level

Then the maintenance starts

Wood's price advantage has a meter running on it. Pressure-treated pine needs staining/sealing every ~2 years — about $1,000 per cycle on a 400-sqft deck when hired out (industry estimate). Composite needs a seasonal wash: ~$100/yr in soap and water. And at year 12, the wood deck's lifespan ends — budget a full rebuild.

Wood: $1,000 stain every 2 years + $7,200 rebuild at year 12 (10–15 yr lifespan). Composite: $100/yr wash, 25–30 yr lifespan, no rebuild. Bottom line: if you'll own the home past ~year 11, composite is the cheaper deck — before counting the weekends you never spend staining it. If you're selling within ~7 years, wood's lower day-one price wins and you hand the maintenance bill to the next owner.

When wood still wins

  • Short ownership. Selling within ~7 years? Wood's $5,200 day-one advantage never gets caught.
  • DIY staining. Doing the staining yourself (~$150–$250 in materials per cycle vs. ~$1,000 hired) cuts wood's 25-year total dramatically — though not below composite's.
  • DIY build. Wood is far more forgiving to cut, fasten, and fix than composite.
  • You love the look. Honest preference counts — just budget the staining instead of pretending it won't happen.

When composite wins

  • Staying 10+ years. The math above is the whole argument.
  • You hate maintenance. A wash with soap and water, twice a year, is the entire job.
  • Kids and bare feet. No splinters, no warping, no popped nails — composite's surface stays consistent.
  • Resale. Remodeling's Cost vs. Value: composite deck additions recoup ~64.5% at resale, essentially tied with wood's 63.9% — but buyers notice a maintenance-free deck.

Railings are the line item that shocks people: code requires them on any deck more than 30 inches off the ground, and at $30–$60/LF they can add $2,000–$4,000 to a mid-size deck — sometimes more than the decking material upgrade from wood to composite. When comparing quotes, make sure railings are in both numbers; a quote that "forgets" them isn't cheaper, it's incomplete.

DIY materials estimated from retail lumber/composite pricing; no contractor markup. Budgeting estimates, not quotes.

Decks are structural — they hold people, furniture, and hot tubs above the ground — which makes this a different DIY decision than a fence. The honest breakdown: wood is forgiving (cut it, screw it, fix it), while composite is fussy (hidden fasteners, exact gapping for expansion, no face-screwing). The permit and inspection process is actually your friend here: the inspector checks footings, ledger attachment, and joist spans — the three things that matter most. If you DIY, pull the permit anyway; it's the cheapest structural engineer you'll ever hire. For the full labor, permit, and inspection walkthrough, see the deck building cost guide.

Resale and insurance: the costs nobody puts in the table

Two financial angles live outside the 25-year amortization. Resale: Remodeling's Cost vs. Value data has composite and wood deck additions recouping nearly the same share at sale (~64% vs. ~64%) — but buyers perceive composite as an upgrade, and a weathered wood deck can read as a project while a clean composite deck reads as a feature. If you're selling in year 8–12, composite's appearance advantage is worth real money that the amortization table doesn't capture.

Insurance: tell your insurer about any new deck — most policies want it listed as an attached structure, and an unlisted deck can complicate a claim. Wood decks in wildfire-prone areas increasingly draw scrutiny (and in some markets, higher premiums or mitigation requirements); composite's fire ratings are part of what you're buying. Neither of these flips the math on its own, but both tilt the same direction the 25-year table already points.

Two forces move the crossover year. In high-labor regions (Northeast, West Coast), every staining cycle costs more, so wood's meter runs faster and composite pulls ahead around year 10. In the hot, humid South, wood's lifespan shortens — moisture and sun punish an unsealed board — but labor is cheaper, so the crossover lands around year 12 anyway. The Midwest is the friendliest market for wood: cheap labor keeps staining affordable, and the crossover stretches to ~year 12. Nowhere does wood win past year 13 on a 400-sqft deck at hired-out maintenance rates; the only way wood stays ahead is DIY staining, which roughly halves the maintenance bill.

The brand matters less than the tier. Every major maker sells a capped board (a protective polymer shell over a composite core) at every tier now — the uncapped boards that gave composite its fading reputation are mostly gone from the market. What the premium tier buys is deeper color, more realistic wood grain, and a longer warranty, not a different maintenance schedule: a value board and a premium board both need the same twice-a-year wash. If the budget is tight, a value-tier composite still beats wood on the 25-year math by roughly the same margin, because the savings come from skipping the staining meter, not from the board itself. One genuine upgrade worth paying for: hidden fasteners. They cost ~$1–$2/sq ft more than face screws, but they eliminate the popped-screw problem and the barefoot test is noticeably better.

The structural costs quotes often hide

ItemTypical costNotes
Framing lumber (joists, beams, posts)$4–$7/sq ftPressure-treated; composite decks use the same wood frame underneath
Footings / piers$150–$300 each6–10 per 400-sq-ft deck, more on slopes or tall decks
Ledger board + flashing$200–$500The leak point on attached decks — never skip the flashing
Hardware & fasteners$300–$800Hangers, bolts, hidden-fastener clips for composite
Joist tape / frame protection$100–$250Cheap insurance against joist rot under any decking

These line items are the reason two quotes for the "same" deck can differ by thousands. A $12,400 composite quote that excludes footings and demo is not comparable to a $7,200 wood quote that includes them — and the cheaper-looking number usually turns out to be the incomplete one. When you compare wood vs. composite, insist both quotes carry the same structural scope; otherwise you're comparing a deck to a pile of boards. The one structural difference worth knowing: composite is heavier than wood, so on a tall or multi-level deck the footing count can tick up — a small adder, usually a few hundred dollars, and your contractor should flag it before you sign, not after.

When to build: seasonal timing

Deck builders are busiest from March through June, when every homeowner has the same idea at once. In that window, expect the longest lead times (4–8 weeks in many markets) and the least flexible pricing — crews are booked and they know it. The shoulder seasons are the buyer's market: late fall and winter in cold climates, when crews are hungry for work, can shave roughly 5–10% off labor, and your start date is days away instead of weeks. The trade-off is weather — footings can't be poured in frozen ground, and stain needs dry days above ~50°F, so a wood deck built in November may wait until spring for its first seal coat.

Composite has a timing edge here: it doesn't need a dry curing window, and supply lead times on popular colors can stretch to 2–4 weeks in peak season, so ordering boards in the off-season locks both price and availability. Either way, get the permit filed before the rush — permit offices slow down exactly when builders speed up, and a 2-week permit wait in April is a 6-week wait in May.

Quote checklist: 8 questions before you sign

Print this and take it to every estimate. A contractor who answers all eight cleanly is a contractor you can trust with the comparison.

  1. Are footings, ledger, and flashing in the price? The structural scope is where quotes diverge most.
  2. Are railings and stairs included — and at what linear footage? Railings alone can add $2,000–$4,000.
  3. Is the permit included, and who files it? Budget $150–$500 if it's on you.
  4. Does the price include demo and disposal of the old deck? Often a $500–$1,500 surprise.
  5. Which exact brand and line is quoted? "Composite" is not a spec — Trex Transcend and Trex Enhance Basics are different prices.
  6. Hidden fasteners or face screws? Hidden fasteners add ~$1–$2/sq ft and are worth it on composite.
  7. What warranties cover the work — material and labor, separately? Board warranties run 25–50 years; labor warranties are usually 1–5.
  8. What is the payment schedule? A reasonable pattern is a deposit, a mid-project draw, and the balance on completion — never the full amount up front.

Composite vs. wood FAQs

Is a composite deck cheaper than wood over 25 years?

Yes — by about $11,500 on a 400-sqft deck ($14,900 vs. $26,400). Wood's total overtakes composite's around year 11.

Does composite decking fade?

Early generations did noticeably; modern capped composites hold color far better, and premium lines (e.g., Trex Transcend) carry 50-year fade warranties. Some mellowing in the first months is normal.

Is composite slippery when wet?

Textured modern boards are comparable to wood when wet. Any deck — wood or composite — gets slick with algae or ice; keep it clean.

How much do railings add to a deck?

A lot: $30–$60 per linear foot installed, and code requires them above 30" of height. A 400-sq-ft deck can carry 60+ linear feet of rail — $2,000–$4,000. Always confirm railings are included in both quotes you're comparing.

Is cedar a good middle ground?

On day one, yes — $22–$30/sq ft installed sits between pine and composite, and it looks fantastic. Over 25 years, less so: cedar still needs staining every 2–3 years, so its lifetime total lands closer to pine than to composite. Buy cedar for the look, not the savings.

Can I build a deck myself?

Wood, yes — it's forgiving and very DIY-able (4–8 weekends for 400 sq ft). Composite is fussier (hidden fasteners, expansion gapping). Either way, pull the permit: the inspector checks footings, ledger attachment, and joist spans — the three things that matter most.

Do I need a permit for a deck?

Almost always for attached or elevated decks ($150–$500). Unpermitted decks get flagged at resale inspection and by insurers — the permit is the cheapest structural review you'll ever buy.

How long does a composite deck last?

25–30 years for most capped composite boards, often longer — premium lines carry 25–50-year fade and stain warranties. Pressure-treated wood lasts 10–15 years before a rebuild. The warranty covers the boards, not the framing underneath, so keep the structure sound.

Does a composite deck add value to a home?

About 64.5% of the cost comes back at resale (Remodeling's Cost vs. Value), essentially tied with wood's 63.9%. The real edge is perception: buyers see a maintenance-free deck as a feature, while a weathered wood deck reads as a project. Appraisers count square footage, not material — don't expect the full build cost back.
