Composite vs. Wood Deck: 25-Year Head-to-Head

CostMyProject Research Team Prices checked October 2026

The most expensive deck argument on the internet, settled with arithmetic. A 400-sqft composite deck costs $5,200 more on day one — and finishes 25 years $11,500 cheaper than pressure-treated wood. Here's the year-by-year math.

Day one: 400-sqft deck, pro install, ground-level

Then the maintenance starts

Wood's price advantage has a meter running on it. Pressure-treated pine needs staining/sealing every ~2 years — about $1,000 per cycle on a 400-sqft deck when hired out (industry estimate). Composite needs a seasonal wash: ~$100/yr in soap and water. And at year 12, the wood deck's lifespan ends — budget a full rebuild.

Wood: $1,000 stain every 2 years + $7,200 rebuild at year 12 (10–15 yr lifespan). Composite: $100/yr wash, 25–30 yr lifespan, no rebuild. Maintenance figures are hired-out industry estimates — DIY staining roughly halves wood's maintenance cost but costs your weekends. The exact crossover year moves with your staining prices; the direction doesn't.

Bottom line: if you'll own the home past ~year 11, composite is the cheaper deck — before counting the weekends you never spend staining it. If you're selling within ~7 years, wood's lower day-one price wins and you hand the maintenance bill to the next owner.

When wood still wins

  • Short ownership. Selling within ~7 years? Wood's $5,200 day-one advantage never gets caught.
  • DIY staining. Doing the staining yourself (~$150–$250 in materials per cycle vs. ~$1,000 hired) cuts wood's 25-year total dramatically — though not below composite's.
  • DIY build. Wood is far more forgiving to cut, fasten, and fix than composite.
  • You love the look. Honest preference counts — just budget the staining instead of pretending it won't happen.

When composite wins

  • Staying 10+ years. The math above is the whole argument.
  • You hate maintenance. A wash with soap and water, twice a year, is the entire job.
  • Kids and bare feet. No splinters, no warping, no popped nails — composite's surface stays consistent.
  • Resale. Remodeling's Cost vs. Value: composite deck additions recoup ~64.5% at resale, essentially tied with wood's 63.9% — but buyers notice a maintenance-free deck.

Railings are the line item that shocks people: code requires them on any deck more than 30 inches off the ground, and at $30–$60/LF they can add $2,000–$4,000 to a mid-size deck — sometimes more than the decking material upgrade from wood to composite. When comparing quotes, make sure railings are in both numbers; a quote that "forgets" them isn't cheaper, it's incomplete.

DIY materials estimated from retail lumber/composite pricing; no contractor markup. Budgeting estimates, not quotes.

Decks are structural — they hold people, furniture, and hot tubs above the ground — which makes this a different DIY decision than a fence. The honest breakdown: wood is forgiving (cut it, screw it, fix it), while composite is fussy (hidden fasteners, exact gapping for expansion, no face-screwing). The permit and inspection process is actually your friend here: the inspector checks footings, ledger attachment, and joist spans — the three things that matter most. If you DIY, pull the permit anyway; it's the cheapest structural engineer you'll ever hire. For the full labor, permit, and inspection walkthrough, see the deck building cost guide.

Resale and insurance: the costs nobody puts in the table

Two financial angles live outside the 25-year amortization. Resale: Remodeling's Cost vs. Value data has composite and wood deck additions recouping nearly the same share at sale (~64% vs. ~64%) — but buyers perceive composite as an upgrade, and a weathered wood deck can read as a project while a clean composite deck reads as a feature. If you're selling in year 8–12, composite's appearance advantage is worth real money that the amortization table doesn't capture.

Insurance: tell your insurer about any new deck — most policies want it listed as an attached structure, and an unlisted deck can complicate a claim. Wood decks in wildfire-prone areas increasingly draw scrutiny (and in some markets, higher premiums or mitigation requirements); composite's fire ratings are part of what you're buying. Neither of these flips the math on its own, but both tilt the same direction the 25-year table already points.

Composite vs. wood FAQs

Is a composite deck cheaper than wood over 25 years?

Yes — by about $11,500 on a 400-sqft deck ($14,900 vs. $26,400). Wood's total overtakes composite's around year 11.

Does composite decking fade?

Early generations did noticeably; modern capped composites hold color far better, and premium lines (e.g., Trex Transcend) carry 50-year fade warranties. Some mellowing in the first months is normal.

Is composite slippery when wet?

Textured modern boards are comparable to wood when wet. Any deck — wood or composite — gets slick with algae or ice; keep it clean.

How much do railings add to a deck?

A lot: $30–$60 per linear foot installed, and code requires them above 30" of height. A 400-sq-ft deck can carry 60+ linear feet of rail — $2,000–$4,000. Always confirm railings are included in both quotes you're comparing.

Is cedar a good middle ground?

On day one, yes — $22–$30/sq ft installed sits between pine and composite, and it looks fantastic. Over 25 years, less so: cedar still needs staining every 2–3 years, so its lifetime total lands closer to pine than to composite. Buy cedar for the look, not the savings.

Can I build a deck myself?

Wood, yes — it's forgiving and very DIY-able (4–8 weekends for 400 sq ft). Composite is fussier (hidden fasteners, expansion gapping). Either way, pull the permit: the inspector checks footings, ledger attachment, and joist spans — the three things that matter most.

Do I need a permit for a deck?

Almost always for attached or elevated decks ($150–$500). Unpermitted decks get flagged at resale inspection and by insurers — the permit is the cheapest structural review you'll ever buy.
