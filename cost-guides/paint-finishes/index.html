Paint finishes explained

CostMyProject Research Team Reviewed September 2026

Sheen is the only paint decision that's both aesthetic and financial: the wrong finish in the wrong room means repainting years early. Here's the no-nonsense version.

The five finishes, ranked by shine

FinishShineWashabilityHides flaws?Use it on
Flat / matteNoneLowBestCeilings, low-traffic bedrooms, imperfect walls
EggshellSoft glowGoodGoodLiving rooms, dining rooms, hallways
SatinSilkyVery goodFairKids' rooms, kitchens, bathrooms, exterior siding
Semi-glossNoticeable shineExcellentPoorTrim, doors, cabinets, window frames
High-glossMirror-likeExcellentNone — shows everythingFront doors, furniture, accent millwork

The one rule that matters

Shinier = tougher, but less forgiving Gloss comes from resins that form a harder, more washable film — but that same reflectivity spotlights every bump, roller mark, and patch. Flat paint is the opposite: it forgives bad walls and punishes sticky fingers. Pick your tradeoff per room, not per house.

Room-by-room cheat sheet

  • Ceilings: flat, always. Anything shinier turns your ceiling into a topographical map of every drywall seam.
  • Living room / bedrooms: eggshell is the default; satin if you have kids, pets, or a hallway that takes abuse.
  • Kitchen / bathroom: satin minimum — moisture and grease demand washability. (Bath-specific formulations exist; they're worth it in shower-adjacent zones.)
  • Trim, doors, cabinets: semi-gloss. It's the standard because it works: scuff-resistant, wipeable, and the contrast against matte walls looks intentional.
  • Front door: high-gloss or semi-gloss. It's the handshake of your house; make it durable.

Does sheen change the cost?

At the register, almost never: brands price every sheen of a product line the same per gallon (a gallon of Behr Premium Plus is $43.98 whether it's flat or semi-gloss). The real cost difference is prep. Semi-gloss on trim demands sanded-smooth surfaces and careful brushwork — the labor to make gloss look good is where the money goes. Flat paint on a lumpy wall is the budget move that still looks expensive.

One more cost angle: higher-traffic rooms painted in flat will need repainting sooner. Spending the same $44 on satin for a hallway can easily buy you 3–4 extra years — the cheapest paint is the one you don't rebuy.

Worked example: the lifetime cost of the wrong finish

Take a hallway — 400 sq ft of wall, repainted DIY at ~$138 a pop (2 gallons @ $44 + $50 supplies). Watch what the finish choice does over 20 years:

The punchline: the "expensive" satin strategy costs $40 more today and roughly halves your repaint frequency. Over 20 years that's one $275 job versus three $235 jobs — plus two weekends of your life back. The cheapest finish per gallon is rarely the cheapest finish per decade.

This is the table that explains every "why is the quote so high" conversation about trim. The semi-gloss paint costs the same $44 as the flat — the $200–$400 of prep is what makes glossy trim expensive. When a contractor quotes trim high, they're pricing the sanding, not the sheen.

Exterior sheens: different rules outside

SurfaceRecommended sheenWhy
SidingFlat or satinFlat hides siding imperfections; satin sheds weather better and lasts longer — the durability tradeoff is real outdoors
Exterior trim / shuttersSatin or semi-glossContrast against siding + washability; semi-gloss on trim is the classic curb-appeal combo
Front doorSemi-gloss or high-glossMaximum durability for the highest-touch surface on the house
Deck / porch floorFlat (porch & floor enamel)Gloss + foot traffic = slippery; use dedicated floor coatings, not wall paint

Outdoors, the washability argument gets stronger and the flaw-hiding argument gets weaker — sunlight is brutally honest about lap marks but weather is brutally honest about cheap paint. Satin is the exterior workhorse for exactly this reason. For the full exterior cost picture, see the exterior painting cost guide.

DIY vs. hire a pro: sheen mistakes are expensive

Sheen errors have an asymmetric cost. Pick too flat for a busy hallway and you repaint in 4 years instead of 10 — annoying but fixable. Pick semi-gloss for lumpy walls and every flaw is spotlighted until you re-prep and repaint the whole room — that's the expensive mistake, because the fix is labor, not paint.

The DIY-safe playbook: flat on ceilings (always), eggshell or satin on walls, semi-gloss on trim. Deviate from it only where you have a reason and the prep budget to match. If you're hiring pros, specify sheens in the contract — "eggshell walls, semi-gloss trim" in writing beats a verbal understanding when the crew shows up with whatever's on the truck.

Three sheen mistakes that cost real money

  1. Flat paint in the hallway. It looks perfect for six months, then the scuffs arrive and you can't wipe them without burnishing the finish. Repaint at year 4 instead of year 10: ~$235 wasted plus a weekend.
  2. Semi-gloss on unprepped walls. Every patch, roller edge, and drywall seam becomes visible under the shine. The fix isn't another coat — it's sanding the room smooth and starting over, which is why this mistake costs $200–$400 in labor, not $44 in paint.
  3. Wall paint on the porch floor. Wall sheens aren't formulated for foot traffic or standing water — they peel within a season or two. Porch and floor enamel (flat, made for the job) costs about the same per gallon and actually survives.

All three share a root cause: choosing the sheen for how it looks in the can instead of how it lives in the room. The finish is a performance spec disguised as a style choice — spec it like one.

FAQs

What's the best paint finish for living room walls?

Eggshell for most living rooms — it hides minor imperfections while wiping clean. Step up to satin for high-traffic family rooms.

Can I mix finishes in one room?

Yes, and you should: the classic combo is flat or eggshell walls with semi-gloss trim. Same color family, different sheens — it adds depth without adding color risk.

Does a glossier finish need more coats?

Not more coats — better prep. The paint covers the same; the shine just reveals what's underneath. Budget the extra effort into sanding and patching, not extra gallons.

What sheen is best for exterior siding?

Flat or satin. Flat hides siding imperfections best; satin sheds weather better and lasts longer. Satin is the workhorse choice — the durability tradeoff is real outdoors, where sunlight and rain punish cheap finishes.

Can I use the same sheen everywhere to save money?

The paint costs the same per gallon, so there's no material saving — and you'll pay later. All-flat in a hallway means repainting in 4 years instead of 10; all-semi-gloss spotlights every wall flaw. The classic split (flat ceilings, eggshell/satin walls, semi-gloss trim) exists because it's the cheapest per decade.

Why does semi-gloss trim cost more if the paint is the same price?

You're paying for prep, not paint: semi-gloss demands sanded-smooth, deglossed surfaces, which adds roughly $200–$400 in labor per room over flat. The $44 gallon is identical — the sanding is the product.

What finish should I use on a front door?

Semi-gloss or high-gloss — maximum durability for the highest-touch surface on the house, and the shine makes the entry look intentional. Budget extra prep: doors show flaws mercilessly, so sand thoroughly and consider removing the door to paint it flat.
