Moving costs in Illinois

Moving in Illinois? Local moves run $1,300–$2,750 for a 2-bedroom home, while long-distance moves price by weight and distance — a 2-bedroom going 1,000+ miles runs $3,850–$7,150. Below: the full tables, what drives Illinois prices, and how to pay less.

The 30-second version Under 100 miles, Illinois movers bill by the hour (2-mover crews ~$80–$100/hr nationally, higher in Illinois's big metros). Over 100 miles, it's flat weight-and-distance pricing under federal FMCSA rules. Illinois moves price ~10% above national averages in our model — see the methodology note below.

Worked example: 2-bedroom local move in Illinois

How the table range is built — a 2-bedroom apartment moving across Chicago (~25 miles):

  • Crew: 3 movers × ~7 hours (load, drive, unload)
  • Effective all-in rate: ~$96/hr (crew + truck + standard equipment, Illinois-adjusted)
  • Estimated total: 21 crew-hours × $96 ≈ $2,025 — the midpoint of the $1,300–$2,750 range above
  • Your planning range (±25%): $1,500–$2,550

Stairs, long carries, packing services, or a month-end date push you toward the top of the range; a ground-floor unit with everything boxed and a mid-month date pulls toward the bottom. This is a budgeting estimate, not a quote — confirm with a binding written estimate.

What drives the price in Illinois

  • Chicago high-rise logistics — Downtown and lakefront buildings require freight-elevator reservations and COIs, with strict move windows. The building's rules run the job as much as the mover does.
  • Month-end lease turnover — Chicago's rental market turns over hard at month-end — the last weekend of the month is the most expensive time to move in the city.
  • Winter: cheapest with an asterisk — November–March brings the lowest prices and the highest weather risk. Lake-effect snow can shut down a move day entirely.
  • Parking permits in the city — Chicago requires permits for moving trucks in many areas — sort it ahead or pay in tickets and carry time.
  • Cross-border corridors are common — Chicago-to-Milwaukee, Indianapolis, and Madison are high-volume lanes with competitive carrier pricing — good for quote leverage.

Illinois moving regulations, busiest routes, and Chicago logistics

Illinois moves are really two different markets: Chicago, with its high-rises, permits, and month-end frenzy — and everywhere else, which is refreshingly normal. Here's what changes the price in each.

Who regulates Illinois movers. Moves inside Illinois are regulated by the Illinois Commerce Commission (ICC) — movers need ICC authority to operate legally. Verify the mover's ICC number before booking; Chicago's huge rental turnover attracts unlicensed operators who undercut on price and overcharge on delivery day. Interstate moves fall under federal FMCSA rules — check the USDOT number on the FMCSA site. In a market this big, the license check is the cheapest insurance you can buy.

The busiest routes. Chicago ↔ Milwaukee (~90 miles) is one of the highest-volume short interstate lanes in the Midwest; Chicago ↔ Indianapolis and Chicago ↔ Madison run close behind. Long-distance, the dominant flows are Chicago → Dallas, Chicago → Phoenix, and Chicago → Denver — Illinois has net outbound migration, which creates a useful pricing quirk: outbound lanes are fiercely competitive (carriers want your load), while some inbound lanes price higher on thinner return traffic. If you're leaving Illinois, get quotes from at least four carriers — the spread on outbound Chicago lanes is wider than most people expect.

Chicago high-rise logistics. Downtown, River North, the Loop, and lakefront buildings don't work like normal moves. Most require a certificate of insurance (COI) from your mover naming the building as additionally insured, a freight-elevator reservation (typically a 2–4 hour window), and many restrict moves to weekdays 9am–5pm. No COI, no move — the building will turn your crew away. Sort this two weeks ahead, not the week of: COIs take days to issue, elevator slots go first-come, and a missed window can push your move by a week in busy buildings. When comparing quotes, confirm the mover has done high-rise work — suburban crews encountering their first freight elevator burn hours you pay for.

Parking permits are your problem until they're not. Chicago requires permits for moving trucks in many neighborhoods and around high-rises. Reputable city movers handle permitting as part of the job — confirm it's in the quote in writing. If it's not, you're the one posting signs 72 hours ahead and paying the tickets when the truck gets towed from an unpermitted spot. Suburban and downstate moves rarely need permits, which is one more line item Chicago adds.

Winter is cheapest with an asterisk. November through March brings the lowest prices of the year — and the highest weather risk. Lake-effect snow and sub-zero cold snaps can delay crews, and a January move that saves 20% isn't a bargain if it takes three attempts. The sweet spot is April–May and September–October: moderate prices, minimal weather risk, and crews that aren't yet booked solid. Whatever the season, ask whether tolls are included — I-90, I-294, and I-355 truck tolls add up fast, and some quotes bury them as a surprise line item.

Getting quotes in Illinois: 6-point checklist

Use this on every quote before you sign — it is the difference between a binding number and a surprise on delivery day:

  1. License verified: ICC number for moves inside Illinois, USDOT number for interstate — checked on the regulator's site, not just printed on the quote.
  2. Binding vs. non-binding in writing: a binding estimate cannot rise unless you add items; a non-binding one can. Know which one you are holding.
  3. Building costs included: COI, freight-elevator reservation, and parking permit confirmed in the quote — not left for you to discover on move day.
  4. Tolls included: I-90, I-294, and I-355 truck tolls add up fast; confirm they are in the quote, not a surprise line item.
  5. Valuation coverage stated: released value (60¢ per lb) is the free default and barely covers anything; full-value protection costs more and covers more. Get the choice in writing.
  6. Date comparison: ask for the same quote on a mid-month weekday — if the month-end price is far higher, the date is doing the damage, not the mover.

Get three binding quotes and compare them line by line, not top line to top line. On outbound Chicago lanes especially, the spread between carriers is wide — four quotes beat three. This is a budgeting estimate, not a quote — plan with ±25% around any figure here.

How to save money on an Illinois move

  1. Avoid the last weekend of the month in Chicago if you can — it's the priciest 3 days.
  2. Book the freight elevator the day you book the mover; downtown slots go fast.
  3. Winter moves are cheapest — bundle the savings against a possible weather delay.
  4. On Chicago-to-Wisconsin/Indiana lanes, get 3+ quotes; competition is fierce.

FAQs

What is the cheapest month to move in Illinois?

November through March, with January–February typically the bottom. Chicago winter moves are discounted — weather permitting.

Why is moving in Chicago so expensive at month-end?

Lease turnover concentrates demand on the last weekend of every month. Movers price it accordingly — shifting even a week earlier or later can cut the quote meaningfully.

Do Chicago buildings require elevator reservations?

Most downtown and lakefront high-rises do, plus a certificate of insurance and restricted hours. Confirm the policy before booking anything.

How much does a Chicago to Milwaukee move cost?

It's a short interstate hop (~150 miles). For a 2-bedroom, expect the lower end of long-distance pricing (see table) — and compare multiple carriers, since it's a competitive lane.

Do I need a parking permit for a moving truck in Chicago?

In many neighborhoods, yes. Arrange it in advance — day-of tickets and long carries from legal parking cost more than the permit.

How much do movers cost per hour in Illinois?

Local crews typically bill $80–$120 per mover-hour in Illinois metros in 2026, with Chicago at the top of that band and downstate metros near the bottom. A 3-mover crew at about $96/hr all-in is the planning figure used in the worked example above — get the crew rate in writing, since some quotes split crew, truck, and equipment into separate lines.

Is it cheaper to move out of Illinois or into it?

Outbound lanes from Chicago are usually the cheaper direction: Illinois has net outbound migration, so carriers compete hard for outbound loads while some inbound lanes price higher on thinner return traffic. If you are leaving, compare at least four carriers — the spread on outbound Chicago lanes is wider than on most routes.
