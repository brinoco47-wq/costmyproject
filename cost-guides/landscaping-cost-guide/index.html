Landscaping Cost Guide 2026

CostMyProject Research Team Prices checked October 2026

Full landscaping runs $15,000–$40,000 for a typical yard in 2026 — but the range between a mulch refresh and a hardscape-heavy design is enormous. This guide breaks down per-square-foot prices, front vs. backyard budgets, the hardscape premium, design fees, ROI, and the traps that blow budgets. All figures are October 2026 US installed prices (±25%), labor included at national-average rates. The single biggest cost driver is how much hardscape the design carries — concrete and stone cost an order of magnitude more per foot than plants.

Cost per square foot: the math

Per-foot pricing is a planning shortcut. New installation: $4–$12/sq ft. Renovation: $3–$8/sq ft. Cleanup: $0.50–$2/sq ft. Three things distort the shortcut: small yards cost more per foot (crew minimums, deliveries, and equipment spread over fewer feet), hardscape-heavy designs blow past the top of every range, and difficult access (steep slopes, narrow gates) adds 10–25% everywhere. Use per-foot numbers to sanity-check a bid, never to accept one — always ask for the line items.

The 60/40 rule most designers use: put about 60% of the budget where you live (the back) and 40% where the street judges (the front) — unless you're selling within two years, in which case flip it. A $12,000 front yard routinely photographs like a $25,000 one; a $12,000 backyard just looks unfinished.

Hardscaping vs. softscaping

Softscaping — sod ($1–$2/sq ft), planting beds ($5–$15/sq ft), trees ($200–$1,500 each), mulch ($0.50–$1/sq ft). It's what makes a yard look finished fastest and it's the cheapest way to transform a property. Hardscaping — patios ($10–$25/sq ft), retaining walls ($15–$50/sq ft of face), walkways, fire pits, outdoor kitchens. It's permanent, it's structural, and it's 2–4× the cost per foot. A 5,000 sq ft yard that's mostly planted: ~$25,000. The same yard with a 750 sq ft paver patio and 300 sq ft of wall: ~$55,000. Neither is wrong — but know which project you're buying before the first shovel hits dirt.

One more distinction that matters at bid time: hardscape prices are mostly labor and base preparation (excavation, compacted base, edge restraint), while softscape prices are mostly plant material. That's why hardscape bids vary less between contractors and softscape bids vary wildly — plant sourcing and design time are where landscapers differentiate.

What drives the price up or down

  • Access (+0–25%). A flat front yard with driveway access is the baseline. A terraced backyard behind a 36-inch gate means everything arrives by wheelbarrow — budget the full 25%.
  • Soil and grading (+$1,000–$5,000). Rocky soil, heavy clay, or a yard that slopes toward the house adds excavation, imported topsoil, and drainage work before a single plant goes in.
  • Drainage (+$500–$5,000). Regrading a swale: $500–$1,500. French drains: $2,000–$5,000. Dry wells and sump systems: more. Water problems are the most expensive thing to fix after the fact.
  • Plant maturity (2–4×). 1-gallon shrubs vs. 5-gallon, 15-gallon trees vs. 24-inch box — specimen sizes multiply material costs fast. Small stock catches up in 2–3 seasons.
  • Existing trees (±). Mature trees to work around add care costs (root protection, hand digging); tree removal subtracts $500–$2,000 per tree before the project even starts.
  • Season (±10%). Late fall is the value season — nurseries discount stock, crews are available, and fall planting establishes roots over winter. Spring is peak pricing.

Design fees: when you need a designer

A professional design costs $500–$5,000: $300–$500 for a planting plan, $1,500–$3,000 for a full front-and-back design with hardscape details, $5,000+ for large or complex sites. When it's worth it: any project over ~$20,000, anything with grading or drainage, and any yard where you can't picture the finished result. When to skip it: a straight refresh, or a small yard where you know exactly what you want. The middle path most people should take: pay for the design ($1,500 or so), then bid the installation to three contractors — the plan makes bids comparable, which is worth more than the fee. Many design-build firms credit the design fee against the install if you hire them.

ROI and curb appeal

Landscaping is one of the few home projects that can return more than it costs: studies consistently show 100–200% payback on quality landscape work at resale, and maintained landscapes add an estimated 5–12% to perceived home value. The mechanism is almost entirely curb appeal — buyers decide emotionally in the first 30 seconds, and a crisp front yard frames everything after it. The ROI ranking, roughly: (1) front-yard cleanup and mulch — near-instant payback; (2) foundation plantings and entry beds; (3) sod and lawn repair; (4) walkway and lighting; (5) backyard patio (enjoyment ROI, weak resale ROI); (6) ultra-high-end backyard builds — often negative at resale if they exceed the neighborhood. Landscape to the neighborhood, not past it.

Money traps

  • Skipping soil prep. $2,000 of plants in unimproved clay subsoil is a $2,000 compost pile within two years. Budget the topsoil and amendment — it's the cheapest insurance in the project.
  • Drainage as an afterthought. Every yard has a water story; ignoring it means replacing dead plants and eroded beds annually. Solve water in the design phase.
  • Over-planting. Beds drawn at mature size look sparse on day one — that's correct. Planting at "looks full now" density guarantees a $3,000 thinning/pruning bill in year three.
  • Change orders without prices. "While you're here, could you also…" is the most expensive sentence in landscaping. Every addition gets a written price before work starts.
  • The cheapest bid. Landscaping has the widest bid spreads in home improvement — 40%+ on identical scopes. The low bid usually means smaller plants, thinner base under the patio, or no soil prep. Compare line items, not totals.
  • Ignoring maintenance cost. A $30,000 landscape needs $150–$400/month of care. Design the maintenance you can afford — or that you'll actually do.

Industry context: the National Association of Landscape Professionals sets contractor standards and publishes homeowner resources, Angi's cost data aggregates millions of real project prices, and university extensions such as UGA Extension publish region-specific planting guidance. Landscaping terms worth knowing

The vocabulary that keeps a bid honest:

  • Hardscaping — patios, walls, walkways. The expensive half.
  • Softscaping — sod, plants, trees, mulch. The fast transformation.
  • Xeriscaping — low-water design: natives, rock, drip. Standard practice in the West.
  • Grading — shaping soil so water flows away from structures. Get it wrong and nothing else matters.
  • Swale — a shallow graded channel that carries surface water. The $500 solution people skip before buying the $4,000 drain.
  • French drain — gravel trench with perforated pipe for subsurface water.
  • Drip irrigation — low-flow emitters at plant bases. The right way to water beds.
  • Topsoil / amendment — imported growing medium and conditioners. Non-negotiable on new-construction clay.
  • Backfill / compaction — soil replaced around walls and pipes, compacted in lifts. Skipped compaction = settling.
  • Edging — bed borders. The cheapest way to make a yard look designed.
  • Curb appeal — street presentation. The front yard's economic purpose.
  • Phasing — splitting the project across seasons or years. Landscaping's superpower for tight budgets.

Landscaping FAQs

How much does full landscaping cost in 2026?

Full landscaping costs $15,000–$40,000 in 2026 for a typical suburban front-and-back yard, or $4–$12 per square foot installed. Projects with significant hardscaping (patio, retaining walls) run $50,000–$125,000. A simple refresh is $3,000–$8,000. Price your exact scope with our landscaping cost calculator.

What is the most expensive part of landscaping?

Hardscaping — patios ($10–$25 per square foot), retaining walls ($15–$50 per square foot of face), and outdoor kitchens. On a typical full project, hardscape is 40–60% of the total. Among softscaping, designed planting beds ($5–$15 per square foot) cost more than most people expect.

Front yard vs backyard: which costs more?

Backyards almost always cost more — typically $10,000–$30,000+ versus $5,000–$15,000 for the front. Backyards are bigger and carry the expensive features (patio, walls, irrigation, drainage). Front yards earn it back faster through curb appeal at resale.

How much value does landscaping add to a home?

Quality landscaping typically returns 100–200% of its cost at resale and can add an estimated 5–12% to perceived home value, driven mostly by front-yard curb appeal. Returns fade on ultra-high-end backyard builds that exceed what the neighborhood supports — landscape to the neighborhood, not past it.

Should I hire a landscaper or DIY?

DIY the easy wins — demolition, cleanup, spreading mulch, and planting from nursery stock can save $2,000–$4,000. Hire pros for grading and drainage, retaining walls over 3 feet, irrigation, electrical (lighting), and anything structural. A hybrid approach (pro design + DIY install of the planting) is the value sweet spot.

How long does a landscaping project take?

A simple refresh takes 2–5 days. A full front-and-back install runs 2–4 weeks. Projects with hardscaping, walls, or drainage work stretch to 4–8 weeks, plus design time (2–6 weeks) beforehand. Weather, permits for walls, and material lead times are the usual delays — not the planting itself.

What are common landscaping money traps?

The big ones: skipping soil prep (plants die and get replaced at full cost), undersized drainage (a $500 regrade beats a $4,000 French drain installed after the fact), over-planting beds that need constant pruning, and approving change orders without prices in writing. Get itemized bids and a fixed scope before work starts.

How much should I budget for landscape maintenance?

Budget $150–$400 per month for full-service maintenance on a typical lot, or $50–$150 for mow-only service. Set aside another $75–$300 per visit for aeration, fertilization, and irrigation winterization. A good rule: plan on 5–10% of the install cost per year in upkeep.
