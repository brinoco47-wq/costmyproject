Local vs. long-distance moving costs

CostMyProject Research Team Data checked October 2026

The single most important thing to know about moving prices: under 100 miles, movers sell you hours; over 100 miles, they sell you weight-times-distance. Everything about how to shop, compare, and save follows from that split.

The 30-second version Local = hourly rate × crew hours (national average ~$1,250). Long-distance = flat rate from shipment weight × miles (national average ~$4,300). A move's distance doesn't just change the price — it changes what you're actually buying.

What counts as "long-distance"

The industry line is 100 miles: under it, local; over it — or across a state line — long-distance (interstate). Interstate moves are federally regulated by the FMCSA: the carrier must give you a written estimate, a "Your Rights and Responsibilities" booklet, and arbitration options. If a long-distance mover won't put it in writing, walk away — that's the regulation telling you something.

Why the same stuff costs 4× more at 1,200 miles

Hours → weightThe billing base flips at 100 miles
Days, not hoursYour truck is committed for a week, not an afternoon
MinimumsLong-distance carriers enforce floor charges (~$1,200+)

A 3-bedroom local move (~$1,800 in our model) becomes ~$6,900 at 1,200 miles — same belongings, different economics. The truck, driver, fuel, insurance, and regulatory overhead scale with distance while your stuff just sits there.

How to shop each type

  • Local: get hourly quotes and ask what's included in the rate (truck fee? materials? stairs?). A lower hourly rate with add-ons often beats a higher all-in rate — or the reverse. Compare totals, not rates.
  • Long-distance: demand a binding or binding-not-to-exceed written estimate based on an in-home or video survey. Non-binding estimates can — and do — balloon on delivery day.
  • Both: verify the USDOT number at FMCSA's website before signing anything. It's free and takes 60 seconds.

When each move type wins on value

  • Local + small home: DIY truck rental is the value king — often under $200 all-in vs. $500+ for movers.
  • Local + large home: full-service is worth pricing seriously; a 4-mover crew can finish in a day what takes you a weekend, and the gap is a few hundred dollars, not thousands.
  • Long-distance: containers (PODS, U-Pack) routinely save $1,000–$3,000 vs. full-service on 500+ mile moves, with a storage month built in — the sweet spot for most interstate moves.

The 100-mile gray zone

Not every move lands cleanly on one side of the line. Moves in the 80–150 mile band live in a gray zone:

  • Some carriers bill hourly past 100 miles on intrastate moves, using state tariffs rather than switching to weight-and-distance. A 120-mile move can price either way — get both quote styles and compare.
  • Crossing a state line flips the regime even at short distances: a 60-mile interstate move is federally regulated (FMCSA written-estimate rules apply), while a 95-mile in-state move usually isn't.
  • State consumer protections vary. Some states cap deposits, require written estimates, or mandate arbitration for intrastate moves — check your state's rules, not just the federal ones.
  • Ask explicitly: "Is this quoted under your local hourly tariff or your long-distance tariff?" The answer determines whether the final bill can move — hourly jobs overrun, binding flat rates don't.

What drives the price up or down

  • Season: May–September is peak moving season; expect 20–30% above off-season pricing and book 8–12 weeks out for long-distance.
  • Day of the month: month-end and summer weekends are the priciest days; mid-month Tuesday–Thursday is the cheapest window.
  • Packing services: full packing for a 3-bedroom runs $1,030–$2,200 in 2026 — the largest single add-on. Fragile-only packing (~40% of full) is the middle path.
  • Access fees: stairs $50–$200/flight, long carry $75–$200, shuttle $200–$500+, bulky/specialty items $150–$600+ each. Disclose everything in the survey — undisclosed access is the #1 source of delivery-day surprises. Full list: 9 hidden moving fees.
  • Valuation: released-value coverage ($0.60/lb) is included by law and pays almost nothing; full-value protection costs ~1% of declared value (~$300 on $30,000 of goods).
  • Weight (long-distance): at ~$0.65–$0.90/lb on 800–1,200 mile moves, decluttering is the only discount you fully control — see the worked example above.
  • Timing flexibility: movers discount flexible delivery windows on long-distance moves; a guaranteed date costs more than a 5-day window.

Best time of year to move

  • Cheapest season: October–April. Off-season demand drops 20–30% off peak pricing on both local and long-distance moves, and crews are easier to book.
  • Cheapest days: mid-month, Tuesday–Thursday. Movers' demand peaks on the first/last weekends of the month — sliding off those dates routinely cuts 20–30% off the identical job.
  • Priciest window: Memorial Day to Labor Day, especially month-end weekends. If you must move in summer, book 8–12 weeks out for long-distance; late bookings pay premium rates for leftover crews.
  • Weather wildcard: off-season savings in northern states come with snow/ice risk — confirm the mover's weather-delay policy in writing before you chase a January discount.

Red flags when shopping quotes

  • Non-binding estimate on a long-distance move. Binding or binding-not-to-exceed, based on an in-home or video survey — anything less can legally balloon on delivery day.
  • No survey at all. A price given sight-unseen over the phone is a guess wearing a quote's clothes.
  • No USDOT number — or a number that doesn't verify at the FMCSA website. Free check, 60 seconds, non-negotiable.
  • Deposit over 25%, or cash-only. Modest deposits by card are normal; large upfront cash demands are the industry's brightest red flag.
  • Broker won't disclose the carrier. Brokers must tell you they're brokers. If they dodge the question of who actually drives the truck, walk away.
  • "Guaranteed" price with no paperwork. Verbal guarantees are worth the paper they're printed on — which is none.

DIY vs. pro on each side of the line

Local: a DIY truck rental often totals under $200 vs. the ~$1,250 national average for movers — DIY saves roughly $1,000+ on small local moves and is the value king. The case for pros strengthens with home size: a 4-mover crew finishes in a day what takes you a weekend.

Long-distance: DIY (~$2,195 for a 3-bedroom at 1,200 miles) is cheapest on paper, full-service (~$7,155) is dearest, and the container (~$4,875) is the sweet spot for most interstate moves — no box-truck driving, storage month included. Full three-way math: truck vs. container vs. movers.

FAQs

At what distance does a move become long-distance?

100 miles is the industry line — under it, hourly billing; over it (or across state lines), flat weight-and-distance billing under FMCSA regulation. The 100-mile mark is also where our calculator switches models.

Why are long-distance moves so much more expensive?

The billing base changes from hours to weight-times-distance, the equipment is tied up for days, and carriers carry heavier insurance and regulatory costs — plus enforced minimum charges. Same stuff, different economics.

How long does a long-distance move take?

Plan on 1–3 days regionally, 4–7 days around 1,000 miles, and 7–14 days cross-country for a large home. You'll get a delivery window, not a date — get it in writing.

Can I get a binding estimate for a local move?

Yes — ask for a binding or not-to-exceed flat rate. Most local quotes are hourly and non-binding, which means a slow crew costs you more. A binding flat rate shifts that risk to the mover; expect them to price in a cushion, but the number can't move on you.

Do movers charge extra for stairs?

Usually, yes: $50–$200 per flight above the first at either end, and long carries ($75–$200) when the truck can't park close. Disclose every staircase during the estimate — including the walk-up at your new place, which people forget. Elevator buildings sometimes cost less than walk-ups; ask.

Is full-value protection worth it?

For most interstate moves, yes. It costs roughly 1% of your declared shipment value (~$300 on $30,000 of goods). The alternative — released-value coverage at $0.60/lb, included by law — would pay $30 for a 50-lb TV. Read the deductible and exclusions before you sign.

What's the difference between a moving broker and a carrier?

A carrier owns the trucks and moves you; a broker sells your job to a carrier and takes a cut. Brokers must disclose that they're brokers — if they dodge the question of who actually drives, walk away. You get more control (and accountability) booking a carrier directly; verify its USDOT number either way.
