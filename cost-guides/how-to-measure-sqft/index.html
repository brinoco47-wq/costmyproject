How to Measure Square Footage for Flooring

CostMyProject Research Team Updated October 2026

Every flooring quote starts with one number: square footage. Measure it right and your budget holds; measure it wrong and you're either over-ordering hundreds of dollars of material or stalling mid-install waiting on a delivery. Here's the 10-minute method, including stairs.

The basic method (rectangular rooms)

  1. Measure length × width in feet, wall to wall. Round each measurement up to the nearest half foot — flooring is sold in full boxes anyway.
  2. Multiply. A 12 × 15 room = 180 sq ft. That's your net area.
  3. Add rooms together if you're flooring multiple spaces with the same material.
  4. Add waste: × 1.10 for simple rooms (10%), × 1.12–1.15 for tricky layouts. This is your order quantity — the number that goes into the flooring calculator.

L-shaped and irregular rooms

Don't try to measure an L as one shape. Split it into two rectangles at the inside corner, measure each, and add:

SectionMathArea
Main rectangle16 × 12192 sq ft
Nook rectangle8 × 648 sq ft
Net total192 + 48240 sq ft
Order quantity (12% waste — L-shape)240 × 1.12269 sq ft

Use 12%+ waste on L-shapes: every inside corner forces angled cuts, and angled cuts eat material.

Walkthrough: open-plan living room with kitchen peninsula

Open plans aren't one rectangle — they're several. Sketch the outline, draw the fewest rectangles that cover it, and treat the peninsula as its own small rectangle:

SectionMathArea
Living area20 × 14280 sq ft
Kitchen wing12 × 10120 sq ft
Hallway stub8 × 432 sq ft
Net total280 + 120 + 32432 sq ft
Order quantity (12% waste — multi-section)432 × 1.12484 sq ft

Tip: blue painter's tape on the floor marking your rectangle boundaries keeps the tape-measure math honest — and gives the installer the same sections you measured.

Angled walls and bay windows: triangle math

For a wall that cuts diagonally across your rectangle, or a bay window bump-out, treat it as a triangle: area = ½ × base × height. Example: a bay window 8 ft wide jutting 3 ft out = ½ × 8 × 3 = 12 sq ft. Add it to (or subtract it from) your rectangle total. For a diagonal wall slicing a corner, measure the two legs of the missing triangle the same way and subtract. Curved walls: measure the widest rectangle the curve fits in and bump waste to 15% rather than doing precision geometry on a Sunday.

Closets, pantries, and doorways

  • Walk-in closets: measure and include — they're real floor area.
  • Reach-in closets: include if the flooring runs in; skip the math if the closet keeps its old floor.
  • Doorways between rooms: don't subtract them when rooms share one continuous floor — the transition eats the gap.
  • Under appliances: include the footprint. Flooring runs under dishwashers and fridges (future replacements need it there).

Stairs: measure separately

Stairs are not folded into the waste percentage — they're measured and usually priced per step:

  1. One tread: tread depth × stair width. (Example: 11" deep × 36" wide = 2.75 sq ft)
  2. One riser: riser height × stair width. (Example: 7" × 36" = 1.75 sq ft)
  3. Per step total: 2.75 + 1.75 = 4.5 sq ft × number of steps. 13 steps ≈ 58.5 sq ft.
  4. Add the landing as a plain rectangle.
  5. Add nosing: stair-nose trim is sold per step — count steps, not square feet.

Installers typically quote stairs at $40–$100+ per step (industry estimate), not per square foot — get it as a separate line item, never blended into the room rate.

Waste factors, honestly explained

SituationWaste to addWhy
Simple rectangle, straight lay10%End cuts on each row; the industry standard minimum
L-shape, closets, many doorways12%More cuts, more offcuts too short to reuse
Diagonal / herringbone pattern15%Every perimeter cut is angled; triangles get discarded
Tile (any layout)10–15%Breakage + cuts; patterned tile trends higher
StairsCounted separatelyPer-step math above, not a percentage

Waste factors by material

The table above covers layout; this one covers the material itself — not every floor wastes the same:

MaterialWaste to addWhy
LVP / laminate10%Click-lock offcuts reuse well; standard minimum
Carpet15–20%Seam placement and pattern matching eat material; broadloom comes in 12-ft widths
Engineered / solid hardwood12–15%Nail-down racking, board-end defects, long planks with fewer reusable offcuts
Tile10–15%Breakage plus cuts; patterned or large-format tile trends higher

When material and layout both push high (diagonal hardwood, patterned tile in an L-shaped room), take the higher of the two factors, not the sum — 15% covers both.

Your measuring-tool checklist

  • 25-ft tape measure — the workhorse. A laser measure is faster for big rooms, but verify odd corners with tape.
  • Graph paper or a sketch app — draw each room and write measurements on the drawing, not on a scrap list you'll misread later.
  • Calculator — your phone's is fine; do the waste multiplication at the store counter if you must, never in your head.
  • Blue painter's tape — mark rectangle boundaries on open plans so section math stays honest.
  • Phone camera — photograph every odd corner, angle, and closet. Future-you will thank present-you when the installer asks.

Common measuring mistakes

  • Measuring the room, forgetting the closet. Then the last box runs out 10 sq ft from done.
  • Using outside dimensions. Measure inside, wall to wall — exterior measurements inflate the order.
  • Subtracting fixtures. Don't subtract for kitchen islands, vanities, or tubs unless the flooring genuinely stops at them — pros run flooring underneath.
  • Mixing units. Inches ÷ 12 = feet, every time. A 10'6" room is 10.5, not 106.
  • Ordering exactly net square footage. No waste buffer means the first damaged box or miscut row stalls the job. An emergency reorder costs $50–$150 in delivery plus days of delay — and dye lots can mismatch.
  • Forgetting the stairs in the room total. A 13-step staircase is ~60 sq ft of material plus $520–$1,300 in per-step labor — big enough to blow a "whole house" budget that only counted rooms.

Measuring FAQs

How do I measure square footage for flooring?

Measure each room's length × width in feet, wall to wall, and add the rooms together. Then multiply by 1.10 for 10% waste (1.12–1.15 for diagonal layouts or irregular rooms) — that's your order quantity. Split L-shapes into rectangles, and measure stairs per step, separately. Round every measurement up to the nearest half foot; flooring is sold in full boxes anyway.

Do I measure closets for new flooring?

Walk-in closets: yes, always — they're real floor area. Reach-in closets: include them only if the new flooring runs inside; skip the math if the closet keeps its old floor. When in doubt, include them: the cost of a little extra material is nothing next to a mid-install reorder, and installers measure before ordering anyway.

How are stairs measured for flooring cost?

Per step: (tread depth × width) + (riser height × width), times the number of steps, plus the landing as a plain rectangle. A typical 13-step staircase is about 58 sq ft of material — and installers quote stairs at $40–$100+ per step (industry estimate), not per square foot. Keep stairs as a separate line item on every quote, never blended into the room rate.

What does "10% waste" actually mean?

Multiply your net square footage by 1.10 and order that much. The extra covers end cuts on every row, damaged pieces, and a few boards for future repairs. It's an industry planning convention, not a measurement — your installer confirms the order quantity before you buy. Running short mid-install costs far more than the leftover box: emergency delivery fees plus schedule delays, sometimes with dye-lot mismatches.

Should I round my measurements up or down?

Always round up to the nearest half foot. Flooring is sold in full boxes anyway, and rounding down is exactly how orders come up 20 sq ft short. Rounding up across a 7-room house adds roughly 15–20 sq ft of buffer — cheap insurance, not waste. The waste percentage goes on top of rounded numbers, not instead of rounding.

How do I measure a curved or round room?

Measure the widest diameter and treat it as a square — diameter × diameter — then accept the overage. Curves waste 15–20% because every perimeter cut is custom. For a curved wall inside an otherwise rectangular room, measure the bounding rectangle and bump the waste factor to 15% rather than doing precision geometry.

Will a contractor re-measure if my numbers are off?

Yes — every reputable installer measures before ordering, so your number is for budgeting and comparing quotes, not the final purchase order. Landing within 5% marks you as a serious buyer; landing 30% off just means your early quotes were fiction. Measure carefully anyway: accurate numbers get you accurate quotes the first time.

Can I measure with my phone instead of a tape?

LiDAR-based measuring apps on recent phones are accurate within about 1% on clear rectangles — fine for budgeting. Verify closets, angled walls, and stairs with a tape; phones struggle with cluttered corners. Whichever method you use, apply the full waste factor — phone precision doesn't reduce cutting waste.
