Deck Material Comparison: Wood vs. Cedar vs. Composite vs. PVC

CostMyProject Research Team Prices checked October 2026

The sticker price is the wrong number. A wood deck is half the price of composite on day one — and costs $11,500 more over 25 years. Here's every material compared on installed cost, lifespan, maintenance, and true cost per year of life.

The insight nobody advertises: composite costs $5,200 more than wood on day one for a 400-sqft deck — then wood needs $1,000 of staining every two years plus a full rebuild at year 12. Composite's higher sticker price is recovered around year 11, and it finishes 25 years $11,500 cheaper. The "cheap" deck is the expensive one if you stay in the home.

Resale reality check

2026 Remodeling Cost vs. Value data (via HonestCasa): a wood deck addition (16×20) averages $17,364 and adds $11,099 in resale value (63.9% ROI); a composite deck addition averages $25,623 and adds $16,533 (64.5% ROI). Buyers value the outdoor space itself — premium materials don't command proportional premiums at resale. Build the deck you'll enjoy; don't overbuild for the appraisal.

What the price range really means: tiers inside each material

The $15–$30 spread on pressure-treated wood isn't noise — it's real product tiers. Here's what sits at the bottom, middle, and top of each range (October 2026 planning estimates).

MaterialBottom of rangeTop of range
Pressure-treated wood$15–$20/sqft — basic #2-grade boards, 16" joist spacing$25–$30/sqft — premium/select boards, 12" joist spacing, hidden fasteners
Cedar / redwood$22–$30/sqft — construction-heart grades with knots$35–$45/sqft — clear vertical-grain, the showpiece deck
Composite$30–$42/sqft — value lines (uncapped or basic capping)$45–$58/sqft — premium capped lines with deep grain and 50-yr warranties
PVC$35–$55/sqft — standard colors, basic profile$60–$85/sqft — multi-tone, heat-reflective, premium profiles

The tier that matters most is composite's: value-line uncapped composite (~$30–$35/sqft) can stain and fade like a problem child, while premium capped composite (~$50+/sqft) is nearly bulletproof. The mid-range "standard tier" used in the 25-year table ($31/sqft) is capped composite — the sweet spot for most buyers. Framing note: composite needs 12" on-center joists (vs. 16" for wood), which adds roughly $1–$2/sqft to the frame — already reflected in installed prices above.

Two worked examples

300-sqft pressure-treated deck, ground level (Ohio)

Surface at $22/sqft (mid-range): 300 sqft = $6,600. Add 4 steps ($75 × 4 = $300), 45 LF of wood railing ($40/LF = $1,800), and a permit ($250). All-in: ~$8,950 — planning estimate ±25%. The railing is 20% of the project; cutting railing footage (a lower deck may not need it on all sides) is the biggest single lever.

400-sqft composite deck, raised 4 ft with railing (Colorado)

Surface at $31/sqft: 400 sqft = $12,400. Raised height adds ~+30% on the surface = $3,720. Add 6 steps ($450), 60 LF of composite railing ($45/LF = $2,700), and a permit ($400). All-in: ~$19,670. Same material, but height + railing + stairs add ~60% over the bare surface — the classic budget surprise. Budgeting estimate, not a quote.

The takeaway:
Never budget "deck material × square feet" and stop. Stairs, railing, height, and the permit routinely add 40–70% on top of the surface number. Run the full scope through the deck cost calculator before you fall in love with a material.

Climate notes: pick for your weather

  • Sun belt (AZ, TX, FL): UV and heat are the killers. Composite gets hot underfoot — choose light colors or heat-reflective PVC lines. Wood needs staining every 18–24 months here, not every 2–3 years.
  • Snow country (upper Midwest, New England): Freeze-thaw cycles punish wood joints and cheap composite. Footings below the frost line (36–48") are code, not optional. PVC shrugs off ice-melt chemicals best.
  • Wet / Pacific Northwest: Constant moisture rots wood fast — use ground-contact-rated lumber or skip wood entirely. Composite and PVC thrive here; ensure good under-deck drainage.
  • Coastal: Salt air corrodes standard fasteners — stainless or coated fasteners are mandatory regardless of board material. PVC and capped composite handle salt best.

Deck material FAQs

What is the cheapest deck material in 2026?

Pressure-treated wood: $15–$30/sqft installed. But over 25 years it's the most expensive option (~$26,400 on 400 sqft with staining and a rebuild) vs. composite (~$14,900). Cheapest day one ≠ cheapest over time.

How long does a composite deck last vs. wood?

Composite: 25–30 years (some lines warrantied to 50). Pressure-treated wood: 10–15 years with diligent staining. Cedar: 15–20 years. PVC: 30–50 years.

Does composite decking get hot?

Yes — especially dark colors in direct sun, noticeably hotter than wood underfoot. Lighter colors and PVC's heat-reflective lines help. It's the most common complaint about composite after price.

Can I put composite boards on my existing wood frame?

Sometimes — if the frame is sound, level, and the joist spacing meets the composite's span rating (usually 12" on-center vs. 16" for wood). Have a pro inspect the frame first; a rotted frame under new boards is money wasted.

How much does a 12×12 deck cost in 2026?

A 144-sqft ground-level deck: pressure-treated wood ~$2,200–$4,300 for the surface, composite ~$4,300–$8,400. Add railing (often required above 30" high — budget $25–$80/LF), stairs ($50–$100/step), and a permit ($100–$800). Realistic all-in for a basic wood 12×12 with railing: ~$4,000–$7,000; composite: ~$7,000–$12,000. Planning estimates, not quotes.

Can you stain or paint composite decking?

You shouldn't need to — and manufacturers generally advise against it, since paint and stain don't bond well to the capped polymer shell and can void the warranty. If a composite deck is faded beyond tolerance, check the fade warranty first; some lines cover excessive fading for 20+ years. For uncapped first-generation composite that's grayed out, a composite-specific restorer is the only finish worth trying.

Is composite decking slippery when wet?

It can be — smooth, uncapped composite is the worst offender, and algae growth in shade makes any deck slick. Capped composite with embossed wood grain has meaningfully better wet traction, and PVC lines marketed as "barefoot-friendly" tend to grip best. In rainy climates, choose textured profiles and keep the deck washed; anti-slip strips exist but look aftermarket.

Should I DIY or hire a pro for a composite deck?

Composite is less DIY-forgiving than wood: it needs 12" joist spacing, hidden fastener systems, precise gapping for thermal expansion, and careful cutting (it chips). A first-timer can do a small ground-level rectangle with patience; anything raised, multi-level, or with complex railing deserves a pro. The labor you save (~30–40% of the project) is real money, but framing mistakes on an elevated deck are structural — when in doubt, hire the frame and DIY the finish work.
