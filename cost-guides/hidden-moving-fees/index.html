13 hidden moving fees (and how to dodge them)

Advertisement

CostMyProject Research Team Data checked October 2026

The quote says $2,400. The bill says $3,100. The difference is almost never fraud — it's accessorial charges: legitimate line items the quote didn't include because the estimator never asked. Here's each one, what it costs in 2026, and the question that kills it — plus the federal 110% rule and the broker red flags that separate legit fees from scams.

The 30-second version Get a binding or binding-not-to-exceed written estimate, disclose stairs/elevators/long driveways during the survey, and ask for the company's full accessorial tariff upfront. Those three moves eliminate ~90% of surprise charges. On any non-binding interstate estimate, federal rules cap what the mover can demand at delivery at 110% of the written estimate.

1. Stair carry fee

Typical cost: $50–$200 per flight (industry estimate). Anything above the first flight at either end usually triggers it.

Dodge it: mention every staircase during the estimate — including the walk-up at your new place, which people forget. If the quote says "no stairs" and there are stairs, that's on the disclosure, not the mover.

2. Long carry fee

Typical cost: $75–$200 (industry estimate). Kicks in when the truck can't park within ~75–100 feet of your door — long driveways, gated communities, downtown loading zones.

Dodge it: ask "what's your free carry distance, and what happens past it?" during the quote. Reserve the closest parking or loading dock in advance.

3. Shuttle fee

Typical cost: $200–$500+ (industry estimate). When a full-size tractor-trailer can't reach your street, the mover shuttles your goods in a smaller truck — and bills you for the second vehicle and crew.

Dodge it: ask explicitly whether a full-size truck can access both addresses. Narrow streets, low bridges, and HOA restrictions are the usual triggers.

4. Bulky / specialty item fees

Typical cost: $150–$600+ per item (industry estimate). Pianos, pool tables, hot tubs, large safes, and oversized artwork need extra crew, equipment, or crating.

Dodge it: list every bulky item in the survey. Better: get a dedicated specialty mover's quote for the piano and compare — it's often cheaper than the general mover's add-on.

5. Packing materials markup

Typical cost: boxes billed at 2–3× retail (industry estimate). Movers charge premium per-box rates for materials used on packing day.

Dodge it: ask for the materials price list before moving day, or supply your own boxes and just hire labor. Our calculator lets you price both ways.

6. Valuation coverage gap

Typical cost: full-value protection ≈ 1% of declared goods value (FreightWaves 2026). The free included coverage — released value, $0.60/lb — is nearly worthless: a damaged 50-lb TV pays $30.

Dodge it: understand you're choosing between $0.60/lb (free, useless for valuables) and full-value protection (~1%, real coverage). For a $30,000 household, that's ~$300 — the cheapest real insurance you'll buy all year. Also check whether your renters/homeowners policy covers goods in transit.

7. Fuel surcharge

Typical cost: varies with diesel prices; often 5–15% of the line-haul on long-distance moves (industry estimate).

Dodge it: ask whether the quote's fuel surcharge is locked or floating. On a binding estimate it should be fixed — if it's not in writing, assume it can move.

8. Storage-in-transit (SIT)

Typical cost: $100–$300+ per month per container/vault (industry estimate), plus re-delivery fees. Triggered when your new home isn't ready and the mover warehouses your shipment.

Dodge it: if there's any chance of a date gap, price a moving container instead — ~1 month of storage is typically built into the container price, which is exactly why containers win on messy timelines.

9. Elevator / certificate of insurance fees

Typical cost: $50–$200 elevator fee; COI processing usually free but slow (industry estimate). High-rises often require the mover to file a certificate of insurance days in advance and restrict moves to elevator windows.

Dodge it: call your building management now — COI requirements with 72-hour lead times are the #1 cause of moving-day delays in apartments.

10. Cancellation / rescheduling fees

Typical cost: $100–$300 or deposit forfeiture; some carriers charge up to $500 (Centennial Moving 2026; MoveAdvisor). Most policies kick in when you cancel or move the date within 3–7 days of moving day (iMoving).

Dodge it: read the cancellation clause before you book, not after. Ask: "What's the latest date I can reschedule with no fee, and is my deposit refundable?" Get the answer in the contract — verbal promises from sales reps don't survive to moving day.

11. Furniture disassembly / reassembly

Typical cost: $50–$200 per item (MoveAdvisor); complex pieces (sectionals, Murphy beds, wall units) run $100–$300 (NYC Great Movers). Quotes often assume your furniture travels assembled — it doesn't, unless you confirm it.

Dodge it: ask whether disassembly/reassembly is in the quote per piece. Simple items (bed frames, table legs) are easy DIY with a hex key; save the paid labor for genuinely complex pieces like Murphy beds and wall units.

12. Custom crating

Typical cost: $50–$150 for a large flat-screen TV; $50–$200+ per piece for artwork (MovingCostPlanner). Glass, marble, and framed art get wood crates billed separately from packing labor.

Dodge it: ask which items "require" crating vs. which can go in mirror boxes (far cheaper). If you have several pieces of art, a specialty art shipper's flat rate often beats the mover's per-piece crating.

13. Peak-season and last-minute booking premiums

Typical cost: +10–25% on the whole quote for last-minute bookings (MoveAdvisor). The calendar math is brutal: peak season runs May–September, weekend rates run 10–20% above midweek, and the last week of any month is the most expensive window — moving on a Wednesday in February can save $500–$1,000 versus a Saturday in July (EverydayBudd).

Dodge it: this is the easiest fee on this list to avoid — book 4–8 weeks out and pick a mid-month weekday. If your dates are fixed, at least collect 3–4 quotes; the spread between carriers on peak dates is the widest of the year.

Know your estimate type — and the 110% rule

Fee disputes live or die on one question: what kind of estimate did you sign? Federal FMCSA rules for interstate moves recognize two:

  • Binding estimate — the price is fixed for the items and services listed. If nothing changes, the price doesn't change. It can only be revised before the truck is loaded, and only in writing (HireAHelper; GraceYourHome).
  • Non-binding estimate — a best guess based on estimated weight. The final bill follows the actual weight and services. But there's a federal cap: the mover can demand at most 110% of the written estimate at delivery. On a $4,000 non-binding estimate, that's $4,400 max before handover; the balance is billed later, typically within 30 days (MoneyCrashers; SafeBound).

Many movers also offer binding-not-to-exceed — you pay the estimate or less, never more — though note that not-to-exceed isn't itself a federal regulatory category (Trunk/Lorea 2026). Two more rules that matter: the estimate must be in writing and signed by both parties — a verbal quote is not enforceable — and the mover must give you the FMCSA booklet Your Rights and Responsibilities When You Move (Better Choice Moving; GraceYourHome).

The cheapest quote is the most dangerous: broker red flags

Some "hidden fees" aren't fees at all — they're the business model of a bad actor. Walk away from any mover that:

  • Won't put the estimate in writing — federal law requires it for interstate moves.
  • Demands a large upfront cash deposit — reputable carriers typically take little or no deposit.
  • Quotes far below every competitor — the classic bait-and-switch setup.
  • Won't share a USDOT number or dodges the in-home/video survey.

Red flags via HireAHelper. And ask one question brokers hate: "Do you own the trucks and employ the movers, or will another company perform my move?" — a broker sells your job to the lowest bidder, which is where the surprise fees multiply (Better Choice Moving).

The pre-signing checklist

  1. Get the estimate type in writing: binding, binding-not-to-exceed, or non-binding. Never accept a verbal number.
  2. Ask for the accessorial tariff: "Send me your full list of extra charges and what triggers each." Legitimate movers have one.
  3. Disclose everything: stairs, elevators, long driveways, bulky items, narrow streets — at both addresses.
  4. Verify USDOT: free 60-second check on the FMCSA website. No USDOT, no signature.
  5. Sanity-check the total against our calculator before you sign.

FAQs

What is the $0.60 per pound moving coverage?

Released-value protection — the basic liability included by federal law in every interstate quote. A damaged 50-lb TV pays $30, whatever the TV cost. Full-value protection (repair/replace at actual value, ~1% of declared value) is the real coverage and costs extra.

Can movers charge more than the quote on delivery day?

With a non-binding estimate, yes — the bill follows actual weight and services. With a binding estimate, no, unless you added items. With binding-not-to-exceed, it can only go down. Get the type in writing.

How do I avoid surprise fees from movers?

Disclose stairs, elevators, long driveways, and bulky items during the survey; demand a binding or binding-not-to-exceed written estimate; ask for the full accessorial tariff upfront; verify USDOT before signing.

What is the 110% rule for moving estimates?

On an interstate non-binding estimate, federal FMCSA rules cap what the mover can demand at delivery at 110% of the written estimate. A $4,000 non-binding estimate means at most $4,400 before they hand over your belongings — the balance is billed later, typically within 30 days.

Can a binding estimate change after I sign it?

Only before the truck is loaded, and only in writing. After loading, the original binding price generally stands unless both sides agree otherwise in writing. Added services and impracticable-operations charges (capped at 15% of other charges due) can still appear at delivery.

What are the red flags of a moving scam?

A mover that won't put the estimate in writing, demands a large upfront cash deposit, quotes far below competitors, or won't share a USDOT number. Ask whether they own the trucks or are a broker selling your job to the lowest bidder — brokers are where surprise fees multiply.
