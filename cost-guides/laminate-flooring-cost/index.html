How much does laminate flooring cost?

Advertisement

CostMyProject Research Team Prices checked October 2026

Laminate is the budget wood-look that refuses to die — and modern 12mm planks look shockingly good. Here's the per-square-foot math, the thickness tiers that matter, and the honest laminate-vs-LVP decision.

The short answer

ComponentCost per sq ft
Laminate materials$1–$5
Pro installation labor$1.50–$4
Underlayment (if separate)$0.30–$0.60
All-in installed$3–$8

Laminate and LVP share an installed range ($3–$8 vs. $3.50–$12) because they share an install method — click-lock floating floors go down at the same speed. The entire price story of laminate is therefore the material story: $1–$2 budget planks versus $3–$5 premium ones, with thickness as the visible proxy for quality. A 12mm laminate at $4/sq ft looks and feels like a different product than a 7mm at $1.50 — thicker core, deeper embossing, quieter underfoot — while installing for exactly the same labor price.

Two specs separate good laminate from bad beyond thickness: the AC rating (AC3 is residential-grade, AC4 handles commercial traffic — buy AC4 for hallways and kitchens) and whether the pad is attached (it should be at $2.50+; separate underlayment adds $0.30–$0.60/sq ft and a second install pass). The pattern-repeat issue on budget lines is real and visible: cheap laminate repeats its wood-grain photograph every 4–6 planks, and once you see the repetition you can't unsee it. Premium lines use 10+ unique plank visuals. In the store, lay out six planks from different boxes — if you can spot the twins, keep shopping.

The water rule Standard laminate and standing water are enemies — a dishwasher leak swells the HDF core irreversibly, and unlike LVP there's no drying it out. "Water-resistant" laminate (wax-sealed edges, water-repellent core) buys you hours against spills, not days against leaks. Kitchens and baths: LVP or tile. Everywhere else: laminate is fair game.

This is the comparison shoppers actually need, because the two products look interchangeable in the store and aren't. The one-line version: laminate is the tougher surface, LVP is the safer floor. Laminate's aluminum-oxide wear layer out-scratches vinyl at every price point — big dogs are laminate's best customers. LVP's waterproof core out-survives laminate wherever water appears — dishwashers, ice makers, and muddy paws are LVP territory. Price being nearly equal, the decision is purely about your house: dry rooms with scratch risk → laminate; any water risk → LVP. Mixed house? Many homeowners run laminate in bedrooms and LVP in the wet zones — the profiles are close enough in height to transition cleanly. The full LVP breakdown is in how much LVP flooring costs.

Water resistance: the fine print, plus underlayment

Laminate's marketing says "water-resistant." The warranty fine print says something narrower. Understanding the gap — and the underlayment underneath — is the difference between a floor that lasts 20 years and one that swells in year two.

What "water-resistant" actually means. A spilled glass wiped up within ~30 minutes: fine. A slow dishwasher leak, an overflowing pet bowl left all day, or a wet-mopped floor: not fine. Water seeps into the click-lock seams and swells the fiberboard core — and swollen laminate doesn't dry back flat, ever. Most manufacturer warranties explicitly exclude water damage and prohibit wet mopping; "damp mop only" is doing real legal work in that warranty booklet. This is the entire laminate-vs-LVP decision in one paragraph: laminate wins on scratch resistance and price, but any room with real water risk — bathrooms, laundry rooms, mudrooms, kitchens with ice makers — belongs to LVP or tile. Laminate in a powder room is a bet that nothing ever leaks; it's a bet homeowners lose regularly.

Underlayment: the hidden line item. Laminate with pre-attached pad skips this; everything else needs underlayment at $0.30–$0.60 per sq ft, and it does three jobs. On concrete slabs, a 6-mil polyethylene moisture barrier is non-negotiable — concrete wicks ground moisture straight into the fiberboard. Everywhere, the foam layer cushions the click-lock joints (extending their life) and quiets the hollow click-clack laminate is famous for. In condos and second-floor installs, check the HOA's acoustic requirement first — many mandate IIC 50+ ratings, which means a specific acoustic underlayment, not the cheapest foam. Skipping underlayment to save $150 on a bedroom is the classic false economy: the floor is louder, the joints wear faster, and the warranty may require it.

The two installation rules. Acclimate the planks in the install room for 48 hours — laminate moves with humidity, and installing cold planks from the garage guarantees gaps by summer. And leave the 3/8-inch expansion gap at every wall and fixed object, hidden by baseboard; tight-to-wall installs buckle when summer humidity arrives, and a buckled floor is a full reinstall. These two rules are most of what separates professional results from DIY callbacks.

Labor is the regional variable — materials barely move, because laminate is a nationally shipped box product. On a 1,000 sq ft main floor, region alone swings the installed total by roughly $1,500–$2,500 between the Midwest and the West Coast, which is why three local quotes matter more in expensive metros than anywhere else. Two regional traps to budget for: Northeast and West Coast older homes hide subfloor leveling ($2–$5/sq ft where needed), and Florida and Gulf Coast slabs demand a proper 6-mil moisture barrier — skipping it to save $150 is how a floor swells in year two. If your quote lands far outside your region's band, ask what is different: thickness, AC rating, underlayment, or removal scope are usually the answer, not the laminate itself.

A two-person crew finishes a 1,000 sq ft main floor in 2–3 working days; a single bedroom is a one-day job. DIY runs at a room per weekend — slower, but the same floor. The schedule matters for budgeting because every extra day of prep (removal, leveling) is a day of labor you are paying for, and it is also where quotes diverge: one installer's "prep included" is another's $2/sq ft leveling line. Get the day-by-day scope in writing before the first plank arrives, and confirm the 48-hour acclimation is on the calendar — installers who offer to start the morning the boxes arrive are cutting the corner that causes summer buckling.

The honest way to compare is cost per year, not cost per square foot. Laminate at $3–$8 over a 15-year life works out to roughly $0.20–$0.53 per sq ft per year; engineered hardwood at $8–$15 over 30 years is roughly $0.27–$0.50 — nearly the same annual cost. The real difference is upfront cash and resale: hardwood adds value at sale time in a way laminate does not, while laminate lets you redo a whole main floor now for the price of one hardwood room. Carpet is the only floor cheaper up front, and it loses on lifespan and cleaning. For most buyers the decision is simple: laminate if the budget is fixed and the rooms stay dry, hardwood if you plan to sell within a decade and want the premium back, tile or LVP wherever water lives.

When laminate is cheapest: seasonal timing

Flooring has a slow season, and it is January and February. The post-holiday lull leaves installers with open calendars, and 5–10% labor discounts are common if you can be flexible on dates. Spring and summer are peak — everyone remodels at once, crews book weeks out, and prices firm up. Materials go on sale on a different clock: Black Friday and holiday weekends routinely cut retail laminate 20–30%, so the smart play is buying the boxes on sale and scheduling the install for the slow season. December is the worst of both worlds — holiday rush plus crew shortages. If your timeline allows it, buy in November, install in January, and pocket both discounts.

Quote checklist: 8 questions before you sign

Most laminate quote disputes are not about the floor — they are about lines that were never in the quote. Ask these eight before signing, and get the answers in writing:

  1. Is underlayment included, or a separate $0.30–$0.60/sq ft line? Attached-pad planks skip it; everything else needs it.
  2. Does the price include old-floor removal and disposal? That is $1–$3/sq ft when it is not.
  3. Subfloor leveling — at what $/sq ft, and who decides it is needed? Agree the trigger before work starts.
  4. Thickness and AC rating in writing? "8mm AC4" on the quote, not "premium laminate."
  5. 48-hour acclimation and 3/8-inch expansion gaps in the scope? Both are warranty conditions.
  6. Stair nosings and transitions priced per piece? Stairs at $40–$100+ per step surprise more budgets than any other line.
  7. Warranty — product vs. labor, and is water damage excluded in writing? Read the exclusion, not the headline years.
  8. Payment schedule? A 30–50% deposit is standard; never pay 100% up front.

A quote that answers all eight cleanly is usually the honest one — even if it is not the cheapest. The cheapest quote that dodges the leveling, removal, or underlayment questions is the one that grows by 30% mid-job. Compare quotes line by line, not total to total, and the real price will show itself.

FAQs

How much does laminate flooring cost?

Laminate installs for $3–$8 per sq ft all-in in 2026 (planning ranges, ±25%): $1–$5/sq ft for materials plus $1.50–$4 for labor. A 300 sq ft living room runs roughly $900–$2,400 installed, or about half that DIY.

What is a good thickness for laminate flooring?

8–10mm is the sweet spot for most homes; 12mm+ premium planks look and sound dramatically better. Below 8mm the floor sounds hollow and shows pattern repeats. Also check the AC rating — AC4 handles hallways and kitchens better than AC3.

Is laminate or LVP better?

Laminate has the tougher scratch-resistant surface (better for big dogs); LVP is fully waterproof (better for kitchens, baths, basements). Installed costs are nearly identical ($3–$8 vs $3.50–$12). Dry rooms with scratch risk → laminate; any water risk → LVP.

How long does laminate flooring last?

10–20 years residentially. The aluminum-oxide wear layer resists scratches well, but moisture is the killer — one major leak can total a laminate floor, while surface wear rarely does.

Can I install laminate flooring myself?

Yes — click-lock floating laminate is one of the easiest DIY floors: score, snap, click, stagger, a room per weekend. The two rules: acclimate 48 hours and leave a 3/8-inch expansion gap at every wall, or summer humidity will buckle it.

Does laminate flooring add value to a home?

Modest value at best. Laminate does not lift resale value the way real hardwood does, but a clean, modern laminate floor beats worn carpet or damaged flooring at sale time and helps a home photograph well. Think of it as protecting value rather than adding it — buyers notice bad floors far more than they pay extra for good laminate.

How much does laminate flooring cost per room?

A 120 sq ft bedroom runs roughly $360–$960 installed ($3–$8 per sq ft), a 300 sq ft living room $900–$2,400, and a 1,000 sq ft main floor $3,000–$8,000 — all planning ranges, ±25%, before removal, leveling, or stair work. DIY cuts those totals roughly in half by removing the labor column.
