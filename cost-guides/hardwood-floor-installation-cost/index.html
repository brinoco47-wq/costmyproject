How much does hardwood floor installation cost?

CostMyProject Research Team Prices checked October 2026

Hardwood is still the resale king of flooring — and the priciest mainstream option to install. Here's the real per-square-foot math for solid vs. engineered, which species cost what, and when refinishing beats replacing.

The short answer

ComponentCost per sq ft
Solid hardwood materials$5–$12
Engineered hardwood materials$3–$10
Pro installation labor$4–$8
Solid installed, all-in$12–$25
Engineered installed, all-in$8–$18

October 2026 planning ranges, ±25%. Labor and installed ranges from the site's flooring data; material splits are derived planning ranges. Budgeting estimates, not quotes.

Labor is the great equalizer: whether the plank costs $4 or $11, the installer charges roughly the same $4–$8 per square foot, because the work — acclimation, layout, nail-down, transitions — is nearly identical. That means the material choice drives most of the spread between a $12 floor and a $25 floor, and it's also why engineered hardwood exists: it cuts the material bill nearly in half while keeping the real-wood surface buyers pay for.

Construction and performance are industry-standard facts; cost ranges are October 2026 planning estimates.

The honest decision rule: buy solid if the floor will outlive you and sits over a wood subfloor — it's the only floor that gets better with age because you can sand it back to new five or more times. Buy engineered everywhere else. Roughly two-thirds of US hardwood installs are now engineered, and the reason is physics, not price: most American homes have concrete slabs on the ground floor, and solid wood over a slab is a cupping-and-gapping disaster waiting for the first humid summer. Engineered's plywood core barely moves with moisture, so it goes where solid can't — and at $8–$18 installed versus $12–$25, the savings are real too.

The veneer-thickness rule Not all engineered is equal: check the wear layer (the real-wood veneer on top). 2mm+ can be refinished 2–3 times like solid; under 1mm is effectively a one-life floor. The $3–$5 engineered lines usually carry thin veneers — fine for 20 years of normal use, but don't buy them expecting to refinish in year 15.

Red oak at $5–$8 is the value play and still what most "hardwood floors" in American homes actually are — there's nothing wrong with it, and it refinishes beautifully. The step up to white oak ($6–$10) buys you the look currently commanding resale premiums: pale, wire-brushed, matte-finish white oak is what high-end listings photograph. Beyond that you're paying for rarity and hardness more than durability you'll notice — a $12 walnut floor doesn't outlast a $6 oak floor, it just looks richer doing it. For engineered versions of these species, subtract roughly 30–40% from the material price; the veneer is thin, so the species premium compresses.

Refinishing ranges are industry-typical 2026 estimates; install ranges from the site's flooring data.

If you already have hardwood under carpet or worn hardwood in place, stop and price refinishing first: sand-and-refinish at $3–$8/sq ft is one-third the cost of new solid, and a properly refinished 1950s oak floor is indistinguishable from new. The test is simple — if the boards are flat, tight, and at least 1/8" above the tongue, they can take another sanding. Screen-and-recoat ($1–$2.50) is even cheaper and works when the finish is just tired. The most expensive hardwood floor is the new one you install over a perfectly refinishable old one.

DIY vs. pro

Nail-down hardwood is the least DIY-friendly mainstream floor: it needs a flooring nailer, a compressor, acclimation discipline (72 hours minimum in the install space), and layout skill to avoid H-joints and rack the boards for color variation. Floating engineered (click-lock) is genuinely DIY-able at a weekend-per-room pace — same difficulty as laminate. The middle path most homeowners take: DIY the floating engineered in bedrooms, hire the nail-down for the showcase living areas. One non-negotiable either way: moisture-test the subfloor (a $30 meter). Installing $8,000 of wood over a damp slab voids every warranty that matters.

FAQs

How much does it cost to install hardwood floors?

Solid hardwood runs $12–$25 per sq ft installed and engineered $8–$18 per sq ft in 2026 (planning ranges, ±25%). Labor is $4–$8/sq ft either way — the material choice drives most of the spread. A 300 sq ft living room: roughly $3,600–$5,400 engineered, $5,100–$7,500 solid oak.

Is engineered hardwood cheaper than solid?

Yes — engineered installs for $8–$18/sq ft versus $12–$25 for solid, mostly because the plywood core costs far less than a solid 3/4-inch board. It also installs over concrete slabs where solid can't go. The tradeoff: 1–3 refinishings (with a 2mm+ veneer) versus 5–10 for solid.

What is the cheapest hardwood flooring option?

Red oak at $5–$8/sq ft for materials is the value king among solid woods, and budget engineered lines run $3–$5/sq ft for materials. Installed, the cheapest real-wood floor is budget engineered at roughly $8–$10/sq ft all-in.

Is it cheaper to refinish or replace hardwood floors?

Refinish, by a wide margin: sand-and-refinish costs $3–$8/sq ft versus $12–$25 for new solid. If the boards are flat and tight with wood above the tongue, refinishing gives you a like-new floor for about one-third the price.

How long does hardwood flooring last?

Solid hardwood lasts 50–100+ years because it can be refinished 5–10 times. Engineered lasts 20–40 years (1–3 refinishings with a thick veneer). Both outlast every other floor type except tile — which is why hardwood still commands the resale premium.
