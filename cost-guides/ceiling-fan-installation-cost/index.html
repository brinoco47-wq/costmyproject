Ceiling Fan Installation Cost 2026

Advertisement

CostMyProject Research Team Prices checked October 2026

A professional ceiling fan installation averages $225–$253 in 2026, with most projects landing between $146 and $360. But that average hides three completely different jobs: a $75–$250 swap on existing wiring, a $150–$400 swap with new support hardware, and a $400–$800 new installation where wiring has to be run. This guide prices all three, explains the labor and the fan itself, and tells you when it's actually fine to DIY.

All figures are October 2026 US prices (±25%), national-average rates. The national averages themselves come from 2026 contractor data: Angi's 2026 average is $253 across 9,729 verified projects (typical range $146–$360), and Thumbtack's is $225 (typical range $146–$351). The single biggest cost driver isn't the fan — it's whether wiring already exists. Labor and wiring routinely cost more than the fan itself.

The rate spread matters less than the licensing boundary. A handyman swapping your old fan onto the existing brace box for $120 is a perfectly good deal — that's a one-hour, breaker-off mechanical job. But "new wiring needed" is where the handyman's $60/hr stops being a bargain: unlicensed new electrical work fails inspections, voids homeowner insurance coverage, and is the leading cause of callback jobs we see in the data. When a quote seems too cheap, ask one question: is the wiring already there? If it isn't, pay for the electrician.

Wood-look fans run $100–$400 and metal/stainless $200–$800 — finish changes the look more than the function. One note the big-box aisle won't tell you: most pros don't sell fans, they install them. You buy the fan yourself, then book the installer. That means the "flat $175" quotes you see from pros (typical in most metro markets) do not include the fan — add $100–$250 for a decent one.

The brace box is the line item most DIYers underestimate and most pros insist on. A ceiling fan vibrates and can weigh 25–50 lbs; a standard nail-on light box is rated for a static 35-lb light fixture, not a spinning load. When pros say "your box needs upgrading," that's not upselling — it's the single most common safety correction on ceiling fan jobs, and the part itself costs under $40.

Size rules of thumb that matter more than the table: keep blades 8–9 feet above the floor (use a downrod on tall ceilings), at least 30 inches from walls, and remember that a fan too small just stirs the air while a fan too big creates an uncomfortable draft. For a typical 12×12 bedroom, a 52-inch fan — the most common US size — is the default correct answer.

Hidden riskOld "mystery" boxes that aren't fan-rated; aluminum wiring; no groundQuote should include box inspection

The DIY case is real but narrow: if a fan was already there, the box is already fan-rated, and you're just replacing it, the job is genuinely a one-hour mechanical swap — turn off the breaker, verify dead, swap the mounting bracket, reconnect black-to-black and white-to-white. Where DIYers get hurt is scope creep: "while I'm up here I'll run a new wire to the other side of the room." That's when it becomes new electrical work, and new electrical work is the pro's job. Also note: if your home has aluminum wiring or an ungrounded box, stop and call an electrician — those are discovered-during-the-job problems, not DIY problems.

How long does installation take

A straightforward replacement or swap takes 1–2 hours including testing and cleanup. New installations with wiring take 2–4 hours; jobs that need a new circuit from the panel plus drywall patching can take half a day. Pros usually quote per-fan flat fees rather than hourly — a typical schedule is one fan per hour once they're on site, so bundling two or three fans in one visit splits the $100–$200 minimum service fee across all of them.

This is the most common real-world job: replacing a bedroom light fixture with a fan. The $330 low end is a straightforward market — existing switch leg, easy attic access above, keeping the existing switch. The $710 high end is the same job with a second switch added, old-work box fighting plaster, and a $200 fan instead of a $140 one. Budget $500 and you'll rarely be surprised.

How to keep the cost down

Bundle fans. The minimum service fee ($100–$200) is the single most avoidable cost — two or three fans in one visit spreads it thin. Buy the fan yourself instead of paying a pro's markup, but confirm the model with the installer first so the brace box and downrod match. Keep the existing switch if pull chains or a remote are acceptable — a new wall switch run is $50–$200 of pure wiring labor. Schedule off-season: electricians are cheapest in late winter when demand dips. And get two quotes with the wiring question answered in writing — "existing wiring, swap only" vs. "new wire run" is a $300+ swing on the same room.

Ceiling fan installation FAQs

How much does it cost to install a ceiling fan in 2026?

A professional ceiling fan installation averages $225–$253 in 2026, with most projects falling between $146 and $360. A simple swap of an existing fan on existing wiring runs $75–$250; adding a fan where no wiring exists runs $400–$800 for labor and materials, fan not included.

How much does an electrician charge to install a ceiling fan?

Electricians charge $50–$130 per hour for ceiling fan installation in 2026 (national average roughly $75–$95/hr), with most pros quoting a flat $150–$250 per fan for a straightforward job. Many have a minimum service fee of $100–$200 even for a quick swap.

Is it cheaper to have an electrician or a handyman install a ceiling fan?

A handyman is cheaper ($50–$80/hr vs $50–$130/hr for an electrician) and fine for replacing an existing fan on an existing fan-rated box. Any job that needs new wiring, a new circuit, or a brace box should go to a licensed electrician — handypeople typically aren't licensed for new electrical work.

Can I install a ceiling fan myself?

You can replace an existing fan yourself on an existing fan-rated box in about an hour — that's a legitimate DIY job with the breaker off. New wiring, new circuits, brace-box installation, or anything involving the panel is pro-only: it requires permits, code knowledge, and insurance that DIY doesn't carry.

Does a ceiling fan need a permit?

A like-for-like fan swap on existing wiring generally needs no permit. New wiring, a new circuit, or a new box usually requires one — permit fees run $20–$100, with a final inspection adding $25–$75 in some municipalities.

How much does a ceiling fan itself cost?

Basic indoor ceiling fans run $50–$150 in 2026; mid-range models with lights or remotes run $100–$400; designer or large-diameter fans run $400–$800+. Damp-rated outdoor fans run $75–$700 and wet-rated fans $100–$1,000.

How long does it take to install a ceiling fan?

About 1–2 hours for a straightforward replacement or swap, 2–4 hours for a new installation requiring wiring, brace boxes, or wall switches. Complex jobs needing new circuits or attic work can take half a day.

What size ceiling fan do I need for my room?

Rooms up to 75 sq ft: 29–36 inch blades; 76–144 sq ft: 36–42 inch; 144–225 sq ft: 44–50 inch; 225–400 sq ft: 50–54 inch; over 400 sq ft: 54+ inch or multiple fans. Keep blades 8–9 feet above the floor and at least 30 inches from walls.
