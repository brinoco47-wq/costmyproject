Deck Building Costs: Labor, Permits, Stairs & Railing

CostMyProject Research Team Prices checked October 2026

The decking boards are only half the story. Labor is 40–60% of a pro quote, and stairs plus railing can rival the deck surface itself on a small elevated deck. Here's where every dollar goes — and how to read a bid like a builder.

Pro labor: the biggest line item

Professional deck labor runs roughly $10–$30 per square foot in 2026, depending on height, access, and complexity — typically 40–60% of the total installed quote. One reviewed invoice for a medium-complexity 16×16 deck showed a labor subtotal near $4,900 (site prep $600, footings $800, framing $1,200, decking $1,600, railing $700). Metro areas run higher; simple ground-level rectangles run lower.

Labor figures: Home Depot installer breakdowns and FixUpFirst 2026. Your deck calculator splits every estimate into materials vs. labor so you can see this ratio on your own numbers.

DIY savings are real but framing mistakes are structural — if any part of the build makes you nervous, that's the part to hire out. A hybrid approach (DIY the demolition and staining, pro the framing) is common.

6 quote red flags

  1. No license or insurance proof. Ask for both; verify the license number with your state board.
  2. No permit plan. "We don't need a permit for this" is sometimes true and often a lie — check your municipality yourself.
  3. One lump sum, no line items. Materials, labor, stairs, railing, permit, and removal should each have a number. No breakdown = no comparison.
  4. Pressure to sign today. Legitimate contractors give written quotes good for 30 days.
  5. Full payment upfront. Standard: a deposit (often 10–30%), progress payments, and a holdback until final inspection passes.
  6. A price far below the other bids. The cheapest bid usually skipped something structural — thinner joists, shallow footings, no flashing. Get three bids; throw out the outlier.

Inspection tips (don't skip these)

  • Footing inspection happens before concrete is poured — the inspector checks depth and size. Missing it can mean digging them back up.
  • Ledger attachment (where the deck meets the house) causes most deck collapses — it needs lag bolts/through-bolts plus flashing, never nails alone.
  • Final inspection covers railing height (usually 36"+), baluster spacing (under 4"), and stair geometry. Failed inspections delay everything.
  • Ask your contractor who schedules inspections — it should be in the contract.

Where you live changes the number

Three geography effects dominate. First, the frost line: in northern states code requires footings well below grade (42"+ in much of the north), which adds digging, concrete, and sometimes helical piers — a cost southern builds simply don't have. Second, coastal and high-wind zones (Florida, the Gulf Coast, hurricane regions) typically demand engineered plans, stainless or coated hardware, and heavier framing — expect the top of the range plus engineering fees. Third, labor markets: metro contractors price meaningfully above rural ones for identical decks. As a planning heuristic, start from the midpoint of the size table, then shift up ~25% in high-cost metros or frost zones, down ~15% in low-cost rural markets — still a budgeting estimate, not a quote.

Metro premiums and code effects: industry estimates compiled by RemodelFinder (remodelfinder.com); footing-depth rules: International Residential Code minimums as summarized by Ergeon (ergeon.com).

Worked example: a 16×20 composite deck, line by line

Here's how a mid-range 320 sq ft ground-level composite deck budgets out, built from the per-square-foot ranges above (CostMyProject worked example, October 2026 — check your own bids against these lines):

  • Permit + drawings: $450
  • Footings (12 piers, below frost line): $1,800
  • Pressure-treated framing lumber: $3,200
  • Composite decking boards + hidden fasteners: $5,900
  • Railing, 60 linear feet composite: $3,300
  • Stairs (one 5-step run): $1,100
  • Labor, framing through finish: $5,500
  • Old deck removal + disposal: $800

Subtotal: $22,050. Apply the standard ±25% planning band and this build lands around $16,500–$27,500 depending on region, access, and contractor. Dropping to pressure-treated boards and a wood railing pulls the same deck toward roughly $13,000–$16,000; going raised adds about 30% (the height premium from the cost-drivers table). Every number here is a budgeting estimate, not a quote — but if a bid lands far outside the band, one of the line items above is where to look.

The 10-year maintenance math

Installation price is only year one. A pressure-treated deck needs staining or sealing every 1–3 years depending on sun and rain exposure; skip it and the boards gray, check, and eventually need resurfacing — which is why "cheap deck" math so often flips by year eight. Composite and PVC skip refinish cycles entirely: soap, water, and a soft brush once or twice a year.

The resale data backs the lifecycle view. Zonda's 2025 Cost vs Value report (the industry's standard benchmark) puts a 16×20 wood deck at $18,263 installed with 94.9% of cost recouped at resale, versus $25,096 for composite with 88.5% recouped. Composite costs about 27% more upfront — but needs none of wood's refinish cycles, and both deck types beat nearly every interior remodel on resale percentage. If you plan to stay 10+ years and value weekends over staining, composite's premium pays for itself in maintenance you never do; if you're selling within a few years, wood's lower upfront cost and higher recoup percentage is the sharper move.

Cost vs Value figures via Ergeon's 2026 summary (ergeon.com); recoup percentages also reported by the Journal of Light Construction. Maintenance cadence: HomeGuide and Better Homes & Gardens guidance as summarized by NerdWallet (nerdwallet.com).

Deck building FAQs

How much is labor to build a deck in 2026?

Roughly $10–$30 per square foot — 40–60% of a typical installed quote. Height, access, and complexity move it most.

How much does a deck permit cost?

Usually $100–$800 by municipality, plus inspections. Engineered permit drawings add $500–$2,500 where required.

How long does it take to build a deck?

A pro crew: 3–7 days for a typical deck. DIY: 2–4 weekends with a helper for a simple ground-level build. Permits and inspections add calendar time on top.

What time of year is cheapest to build a deck?

Late fall and winter — contractors discount when demand drops. You'll wait longer for concrete to cure in freezing weather, but the savings are real.

Does a deck add value to a home?

Yes — decks have among the best resale returns of any remodel. The 2025 Zonda Cost vs Value report shows wood deck additions recouping about 94.9% of cost and composite about 88.5% at resale, ahead of most kitchen and bathroom projects.

Is composite actually cheaper than wood over time?

At installation, no: composite runs about 27% more upfront. Over 20 years it usually narrows the gap because it skips the refinish cycles wood needs every 1–3 years. Staying 10+ years favors composite; selling soon favors wood's lower cost and higher recoup percentage.
