How Labor Rates Vary by ZIP Code

Advertisement

CostMyProject Research Team Prices checked October 2026

Every CostMyProject calculator has an optional ZIP-code field. Enter it, and the labor portion of your estimate is scaled to your local labor market — using real Bureau of Labor Statistics wage data, not a guess. Here is exactly how it works, where the numbers come from, and where it falls short.

Why ZIP codes matter more than you think

A gallon of paint costs about the same in Boise and Boston — national retail chains see to that. Labor is a different story. The BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS) survey found that in May 2023, construction laborers in the Chicago metro earned a mean of $33.35/hour, while in Texas the state mean was $18.74/hour. That is a 78% gap for the same occupation. Any "national average" labor figure is therefore wrong almost everywhere — too high for Houston, too low for San Francisco. Our ZIP adjustment exists to fix that.

Most free calculators ignore geography entirely. The ones that handle it well (notably Homewyse) treat ZIP localization as their core feature. We built our version on public BLS data so the method is auditable: every multiplier below can be traced to a published BLS wage figure.

How the multiplier is built

The input is the first 3 digits of your ZIP code (the ZIP3 area — e.g. 90210 → 902). ZIP3 is the finest geography we can support honestly: BLS publishes wages by metro and state, not by 5-digit ZIP, and mapping 41,000+ ZIP codes to metros would pretend to a precision the source data does not have.

Each of the 912 ZIP3 areas in the US gets one multiplier between 0.80 and 1.40, computed as:

multiplier = area mean hourly wage ÷ $23.69 (the BLS OEWS May 2023 national mean for construction laborers, SOC 47-2061), rounded to 2 decimals and clamped to the 0.80–1.40 band.

When you enter a ZIP, only the labor lines of the estimate are multiplied. Materials, permits, and flat fees stay at national figures. The breakdown shows a Local labor adjustment (ZIP 90210: ×1.23) row so you can see exactly what changed. Leave the field empty and everything computes at ×1.00, exactly as before.

The three data tiers

Not all 912 areas have equally good data, so we label the source on every lookup:

  • Tier 1 — Metro-verified (98 ZIP3 areas). BLS OEWS May 2023 published mean wages for construction laborers in 16 major metropolitan areas, applied to their core ZIP3s. Example: Chicago metro $33.35 → ×1.40; Houston metro $19.08 → ×0.81.
  • Tier 2 — State-verified (294 ZIP3 areas). BLS OEWS May 2023 published state means for 14 states, applied to ZIP3s outside the Tier 1 metros. Example: California $29.67 → ×1.25; Florida $19.56 → ×0.83.
  • Tier 3 — State estimates (520 ZIP3 areas). Planning estimates for the remaining states, calibrated from BLS Current Employment Statistics (CES) state construction-earnings relativities anchored to the OEWS-verified states. Labeled as estimates everywhere they appear.

ZIP codes the table does not cover (military APO/FPO addresses and a handful of unmapped areas) fall back to ×1.00 with a "national average" note — the calculator works fine, it just skips the local adjustment.

The pattern is real and worth internalizing: union-dense coastal and Great Lakes metros run 25–40% above the national average, while right-to-work Southern metros run 15–20% below it. Generic cost-of-living intuition gets this wrong — Washington DC's overall cost of living is sky-high, yet its construction-laborer wages sit below the national mean. That is exactly why we use occupation-specific BLS data instead of a cost-of-living index.

Worked example: one project, three ZIP codes

Take a project whose labor line is $5,000 at the national average. Materials, permits, and flat fees stay the same everywhere, so only that $5,000 moves. Here is what the same job costs in three of the verified metros:

Metro (ZIP3)MultiplierLabor lineProject total*
Chicago (606)×1.40$7,000$11,000
Los Angeles (900)×1.23$6,150$10,150
Houston (770)×0.81$4,050$8,050

*Project total assumes $4,000 of materials, permits, and flat fees on top of the labor line. The point is the spread: $2,950 between Houston and Chicago for identical work. A national-average quote would overcharge the Houston homeowner by about $950 and under-budget the Chicago homeowner by about $2,000 — which is exactly why the ZIP field exists. Treat the adjusted total as the centre of a ±25% budgeting range, not a single fixed number.

How to use the multiplier when you budget

  1. Run the calculator twice. Once with your ZIP, once without. The gap between the two totals is the size of your local adjustment — if it is small, geography barely matters for your job; if it is large, your quotes should reflect it.
  2. Check your tier. Tier 1 and Tier 2 areas use published BLS means, so the adjustment is firmer. Tier 3 areas use calibrated state estimates — treat the result as a wider band and lean harder on local quotes.
  3. Compare against three written quotes. The multiplier is a planning figure. Three itemized local quotes are the reality check; if all three cluster far from the adjusted estimate, the quotes are telling you something the wage data cannot.
  4. Use the ±25% band. Budget the adjusted figure plus or minus 25%. A quote inside that band is consistent with the data; a quote outside it deserves a question, not an automatic rejection.
  5. Re-run if the scope changes. Adding a room, switching materials, or moving the job to a different season changes the labor share — and the labor share is the part the multiplier moves.

What the multiplier does not capture

  • Small-job minimums. Most contractors charge a minimum call-out or day rate. A two-hour job does not scale linearly from an hourly wage, so small jobs often price above the multiplier.
  • Permits and inspections. Permit fees vary by city and county, not by wage data. They sit outside the labor line and are not adjusted.
  • Job difficulty and access. Steep roofs, tight crawl spaces, and high-rise freight elevators change the labor hours. The multiplier knows your metro, not your house.
  • Union vs non-union within a metro. A BLS metro mean blends union and non-union crews. A union crew in the same city can price above the mean, a non-union crew below it.
  • Rural travel. Crews driving 60+ miles to a rural job may add a trip charge the wage data never sees.

Limitations — read this before trusting the number

  • It is a planning estimate, not a quote. BLS means describe employees of construction firms, not the bid price of your specific contractor, who adds overhead, profit, scheduling, and job-specific difficulty.
  • ZIP3 is coarse. Manhattan (100) and the outer Bronx (104) share economics; so do Beverly Hills (902) and East LA (900)? Not exactly — both are "Los Angeles metro" in our table. Within-metro variation (often ±15%) is not captured.
  • Occupation proxy. We use construction laborers (47-2061) as the proxy for all project labor. Licensed trades (electricians, plumbers) have different — usually wider — geographic spreads.
  • Data vintage. May 2023 OEWS is the latest fully published detailed release we could verify at build time. Wages move; the relativities between areas move much more slowly, which is what the multiplier relies on.
  • Tier 3 is estimated. 520 ZIP3 areas use state estimates, not published OEWS means. They are calibrated and clamped, but they are estimates — labeled as such on every lookup.

Bottom line: the ZIP adjustment moves a national-average labor figure meaningfully closer to your market. It does not replace getting three written quotes from local contractors — nothing does.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my ZIP code change the labor estimate?

Construction labor wages vary enormously by metro. BLS data shows construction laborers in the Chicago metro earn about 41% above the national average, while in Texas they earn about 21% below it. Materials cost roughly the same everywhere (national retail chains), so geography mostly moves the labor line — which is the part we adjust.

Where does the wage data come from?

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS), May 2023 release — mean hourly wages for construction laborers (SOC 47-2061), the occupation closest to general home-improvement labor. National mean: $23.69/hour. Every multiplier is that area's wage divided by the national mean.

What if my ZIP code is not in the table?

You get the national average (×1.00) with a note saying so. Military APO/FPO ZIPs and a few unmapped areas fall back to 1.00. The estimate still works — it just doesn't apply a local adjustment.

Does the multiplier apply to materials too?

No — deliberately. Lumber, paint, and shingles are priced by national retailers with modest regional variation, while labor is hyper-local. Scaling only the labor portion keeps the adjustment honest instead of inflating the whole project.

How accurate is the ZIP adjustment?

It is a planning estimate, not a quote. Tier 1 (major metros) and Tier 2 (14 states) use published BLS means; Tier 3 uses state estimates calibrated to BLS data. All values are rounded to 2 decimals and clamped to 0.80–1.40. Real contractor quotes in your ZIP can still differ — always get three written quotes.

Can I use the multiplier to negotiate a contractor's quote?

Use it as a sanity check, not a bargaining chip. If a quote comes in well above the ZIP-adjusted estimate, ask for an itemized breakdown — labor, materials, permits — and compare line by line. A legitimate gap usually has a reason (difficult access, premium materials, a tight schedule). If the contractor cannot explain it, get another quote. Never present a calculator printout as a price demand; contractors price their own costs, not ours.

Why does my rural ZIP get the state average instead of a local number?

BLS publishes construction-laborer wages by metro area and by state, not by county or small town. If your ZIP3 sits outside one of the 16 verified metros, it falls back to its state's published mean (Tier 2) or a calibrated state estimate (Tier 3). Within-state variation — a resort town versus a farm county in the same state — is not captured, so rural estimates carry a wider planning band. Three local quotes matter more there than anywhere else.
