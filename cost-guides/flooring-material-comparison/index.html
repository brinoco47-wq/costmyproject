Deep dive: what each material actually costs installed

Ranges are October 2026 US planning estimates (±25%), pro install with ~10% waste. Every installed total below is material + underlayment + labor — which is why the flooring calculator splits those three line items instead of blending them.

Carpet: $3–$8/sq ft installed

The budget breaks down roughly to material $1–$5/sq ft, pad $0.35–$0.80, and labor $0.50–$1.50 — the cheapest install of any material because stretch-in is fast. Pros: softest and warmest underfoot, quietest, lowest upfront check. Cons: stains, traps allergens, shortest life (5–15 years), and the highest cost per year of use (~$0.55) because you replace it every decade. Best rooms: bedrooms and family rooms where comfort beats durability.

Laminate: $3–$8/sq ft installed

Roughly material $1–$5/sq ft (budget planks $1–$2, premium $3–$5), underlayment $0.30–$0.60, labor $1.50–$4 for click-lock floating install. Pros: cheapest wood look, very DIY-friendly, tougher surface than many expect. Cons: standing water ruins it, click sound underfoot, can't be refinished. Best rooms: dry bedrooms and living rooms where the budget is tight and water risk is zero.

LVP (luxury vinyl plank): $4–$10/sq ft installed

Roughly material $2–$8/sq ft, underlayment often $0 (attached pad is standard), labor $1.50–$4 — glue-down runs higher than click-lock. Pros: 100% waterproof, easy DIY, solid per-year cost (~$0.39), handles basements and kitchens. Cons: can't be refinished, can feel hollow underfoot, no resale premium. Best rooms: kitchens, bathrooms, basements, and anywhere rental turnover takes abuse.

Engineered hardwood: $8–$18/sq ft installed

Roughly material $4–$12/sq ft, labor $4–$8 for nail- or glue-down with moisture checks and careful layout. Pros: real wood surface, tolerates slabs and humidity better than solid, refinishable once or twice. Cons: thin wear layer limits refinishing, only partial water resistance, premium price over LVP. Best rooms: living and dining rooms on concrete slabs or in humid climates.

Ceramic / porcelain tile: $7–$20/sq ft installed

Roughly material $2.50–$8/sq ft, setting materials (thinset, grout) on top, labor $4–$10 — labor routinely exceeds the tile's own price because of precision cutting and multi-day mortar/grout curing. Pros: waterproof, 25–75 year lifespan, excellent per-year cost (~$0.27). Cons: cold and hard underfoot, grout needs resealing every 1–3 years, the most labor-intensive install. Best rooms: kitchens, bathrooms, laundry, entryways.

Solid hardwood: $12–$25/sq ft installed

Roughly material $6–$12/sq ft, labor $4–$8 for nail-down plus acclimation and often site finishing. Pros: 50–100 year lifespan, refinishable 5–7 times, highest resale recovery (70–80%), and the lowest cost per year of use (~$0.22) despite the biggest check. Cons: highest upfront cost, hates water and slab moisture, shows scratches. Best rooms: living rooms, dining rooms, and bedrooms in homes you'll own 15+ years.

Material/labor splits are planning ranges (±25%), consistent with the per-material guides. All are budgeting estimates, not quotes.

The insight most comparisons bury

The most expensive floor per square foot — solid hardwood — is the cheapest per year of use (~$0.22/sq ft/yr), because it lasts 50–100 years and can be refinished 5–7 times. Carpet looks cheapest installed but costs ~$0.55/sq ft/yr because you replace it every decade. Two rules of thumb:

  • Staying 15+ years? Hardwood or tile wins on lifetime cost, and hardwood adds 2.5–3% to resale value (NAR).
  • Selling within 5–7 years? LVP is the smarter spend — waterproof, looks like wood, and you won't own it long enough for hardwood's longevity to pay back.

Resale reality check

  • Hardwood: 70–80% cost recovery, +2.5–3% home value (National Association of Realtors).
  • LVP / tile: 50–70% recovery — buyers like them, but they don't command a premium.
  • Laminate: 30–50% — fine for living in, weak as an investment.
  • Carpet: 25–40% — buyers mentally price in replacing it.

Recovery figures are industry-reported ranges for the US resale market, not guarantees — local buyer expectations dominate.

What drives the price up or down

  • Subfloor condition. Leveling runs $2–$5/sq ft when needed — on a 1,000 sq ft job that's up to $5,000 before a single plank goes down. Older homes almost always need some.
  • Tear-out. Removing old flooring adds $1–$3/sq ft. DIY carpet demo is easy savings; tile demo usually isn't.
  • Stairs and patterns. Stairs are priced per step ($40–$100+), and herringbone or diagonal layouts add 20–40% to labor.
  • Material grade within a category. Budget laminate at $1/sq ft and premium at $5/sq ft are technically the "same material" with a 5× spread.
  • Your zip code. Identical LVP installs vary up to 45% between regions — Northeast and West Coast highest, Midwest and South lowest.

Ways to push the total down: DIY click-lock materials, clearing rooms yourself ($50–$150/room in moving fees), bundling all rooms into one job, and buying mid-grade material in full-box quantities. Every figure here is a budgeting estimate, not a quote.

How to actually use the per-year number

Cost per year of use isn't an invoice line — it's a decision lens. Multiply it by your area and your planned stay: LVP at ~$0.39 × 1,000 sq ft × 7 years ≈ $2,730 of "value consumed" before you sell, versus hardwood's $0.22 × 1,000 × 7 ≈ $1,540 — but only if you stay long enough for hardwood's longevity to matter. Selling in year 5? Hardwood's remaining 45+ years of life walk out the door with the buyer, and you paid for all of them. The worked scenarios on the head-to-head scenarios page run this math for rentals, flips, and forever homes.

Material FAQs

What is the best flooring for the money in 2026?

By cost per year of use, solid hardwood: $12–$25/sq ft installed over a 50–100 year life works out to roughly $0.22 per sq ft per year — the lowest of the six. If you might sell within 5–7 years, LVP (~$0.39/yr, waterproof, easy DIY) is the better buy, since hardwood's longevity payback needs time you won't have.

Is LVP better than laminate?

In wet areas, decisively: LVP is 100% waterproof while laminate swells and buckles with standing water. Laminate is slightly cheaper ($1–$5 vs $2–$8 per sq ft material) and both install as easy click-lock DIY. For kitchens, baths, and basements, choose LVP. For dry bedrooms on a tight budget, laminate holds its own — just keep every drop of water away from it.

Can you put hardwood in a kitchen?

Engineered hardwood, yes — it handles humidity swings far better than solid. Solid hardwood in a kitchen is a maintenance commitment: wipe spills immediately and expect seasonal movement. Most 2026 buyers choose LVP or tile for kitchens instead, reserving real wood for living areas where water never lands and the resale premium is visible.

Does hardwood flooring increase home value?

Yes. The National Association of Realtors reports hardwood adds roughly 2.5–3% to resale value with 70–80% cost recovery — the highest of any flooring material. LVP and tile recover roughly 50–70%, laminate 30–50%, and carpet 25–40%, since buyers mentally price in replacing it. In competitive markets, "hardwood throughout" remains a listing-line advantage.

How long does each flooring type actually last?

Typical lifespans: carpet 5–15 years, laminate 10–20, LVP 15–25, engineered hardwood 20–40, tile 25–75, and solid hardwood 50–100+ with refinishing. Pets, direct sunlight, and maintenance swing these by years in either direction — a well-kept tile floor can outlive its installer, while a rental carpet rarely sees year seven.

What is the cheapest flooring to install?

Carpet and laminate tie at $3–$8/sq ft installed. But cheapest installed is not cheapest owned: carpet costs ~$0.55/sq ft per year (the highest of the six) because it wears out every 5–15 years. For a stay under 5 years, carpet's low entry price wins. Past 10 years, LVP's ~$0.39/yr pulls ahead despite costing more on day one.

Is tile worth the high labor cost?

Often, yes. Tile labor ($4–$10/sq ft) routinely exceeds the tile's own material price — the sticker shock is real. But tile lasts 25–75 years at ~$0.27/sq ft per year, the second-lowest of the six materials. In kitchens and bathrooms, where waterproofing is non-negotiable, that labor buys decades. In dry rooms, LVP delivers most of the durability for roughly half the labor cost.

Does refinishing really change hardwood's math?

Completely. One solid-hardwood refinish (~$2,500 for 1,000 sq ft) resets the floor to like-new for about 15% of a full reinstall — and you can do it 5–7 times over the floor's life. Engineered hardwood gets 1–2 refinishes before the wear layer runs out. No other material gets "new again" for ~$2.50/sq ft, which is why wood's per-year cost keeps falling the longer you own it.
