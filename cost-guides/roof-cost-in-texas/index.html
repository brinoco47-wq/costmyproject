Roof Costs in Texas (2026)

Advertisement

CostMyProject Research Team Prices checked October 2026

A roof replacement on a standard 2,000 sq ft Texas home costs about $15,555 at 6/12 pitch with architectural shingles — $1,565 below the national average of $17,120. The entire difference is labor: Texas construction wages run below the national average, while materials, tear-off, and permits cost the same. This page shows the Texas math, how it compares to the South average, and what the Texas number does not include.

The Texas worked example: same roof, Texas labor

Same roof as our national example: a 40×50 ft colonial, 2,000 sq ft footprint, 6/12 pitch (roof surface 2,236 sq ft), architectural shingles, one-layer tear-off, $350 permit. Only the labor line changes — scaled by the Texas labor multiplier of ×0.80.

How the Texas multiplier is calculated

The ×0.80 figure is the Texas state average for construction laborers from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (May 2023), ratioed to the national mean wage — the same dataset behind this site's ZIP-by-ZIP labor adjustment. In our ZIP3 data, all 49 Texas ZIP3 areas carry the state-average value (range ×0.80–×0.82), so the state figure is stable, not driven by one metro. Materials are not scaled: shingles cost the same in Texas as nationally in this model. Enter your ZIP in the roof cost calculator for your metro's exact multiplier.

What the Texas number does not include

  • Pitch and stories. Steep pitches (+15% at 9/12, +25% at 12/12) and second stories (+10–20%) raise labor on top of the Texas base.
  • Decking replacement. Rotten decking found during tear-off is billed per sheet on top of the quote.
  • A second tear-off layer. The example assumes one layer; two layers roughly double the $3,354 tear-off line.
  • Storm and hail effects. The multiplier is labor-only. Hail history, code upgrades, and insurer requirements show up in individual Texas quotes, not in this average.

The honest way to use this page: treat $15,555 as the Texas baseline for this exact roof, then adjust for your pitch, stories, layers, and decking — or run your roof in the calculator with your ZIP.

Storm damage note: what insurance pays depends on whether your policy settles at ACV (depreciated value) or RCV (full replacement, with depreciation recoverable after completion). Our national worked example — $17,120 roof, 18 years old on a 28-year life, $1,000 deductible assumed — pays a $5,114 first check on ACV versus the full cost minus deductible on RCV. The full math, with assumptions stated, is on the roof calculator's insurance section. Insurers use their own depreciation schedules; this is not a coverage determination.

Hail country: why Texas roofs are different

Texas has been the hail capital of the United States for 11 straight years. According to the Insurance Information Institute, Texas recorded 902 major-damage hail events in 2025 — more than double second-place Illinois at 375 — and State Farm alone paid about $1.4 billion in Texas hail claims that year. That history is why Texas roofing decisions revolve around hail more than in any other state, and why the cheapest shingle is often the most expensive roof over time.

The practical response is a Class 4 impact-resistant shingle: the top rating under the UL 2218 impact test, in which a 2-inch steel ball is dropped from 20 feet to simulate a baseball-sized hailstone. Class 4 shingles typically cost 10–25% more than standard architectural shingles, but Texas law requires insurers to offer premium discounts for qualifying impact-resistant roofing — reported discounts run roughly 10–35% on the dwelling portion of the premium, varying by carrier. Activating the discount requires documentation: your contractor completes Texas Department of Insurance Form PC068, the impact-resistant roofing installation form, and files it with your insurer.

Two caveats before you choose. First, Class 4 means more resistant, not immune: hail large enough will still damage one, and that damage is still a claim. Second, some carriers tie the full discount to a cosmetic-damage exclusion — dented but uncracked shingles may pay nothing. Ask your insurer, in writing, whether the discount you are quoted carries a cosmetic exclusion before you sign.

Coast: TWIA and windstorm rules

On the Gulf Coast, wind coverage works differently. The Texas Windstorm Insurance Association (TWIA) is the state's insurer of last resort for windstorm and hail: it writes wind and hail coverage only, and only in the 14 first-tier coastal counties plus parts of Harris County east of Highway 146. If your private homeowners policy excludes wind in that zone, TWIA is usually what fills the gap — but it is a last resort, so eligibility requires a declination from at least one private insurer, and the home must meet applicable building codes as certified by the Texas Department of Insurance or TWIA.

What this means for your roof budget: a coastal re-roof may need to satisfy TWIA/TDI certification to keep coverage, and a policy that pairs TWIA wind coverage with a separate homeowners policy for fire, theft, and liability is normal on the coast — two policies, not a gap. Confirm with your agent, before the tear-off, which certification your new roof needs; discovering it after installation is the expensive version.

Codes and permits: check your city, not the state

Texas adopts the International Residential Code as the municipal residential building code baseline (currently the 2012 edition under state statute), but adoption and enforcement happen at the local level: cities may amend the code to meet local concerns, and enforcement outside city limits is limited since the state abolished the Texas Residential Construction Commission in 2009. There is no single statewide roofing permit rule to quote. Whether your re-roof needs a permit, an inspection, or both depends on your city's permitting office — check with it before work starts, and get the answer in writing on the quote. A contractor who says "no permit needed here" should be able to name the office that confirmed it.

Texas permit + quote checklist

  • City permit confirmed in writing — permit requirement and inspection schedule from your city's permitting office, on the quote.
  • Hail rating stated on the quote — shingle brand, line, and UL 2218 class (Class 4 or standard), not just "architectural".
  • TDI Form PC068 promised — if you are buying Class 4 for the insurance discount, the contractor completes it; get that commitment before work starts.
  • Coastal certification named — in a TWIA county, which TDI/TWIA certification the new roof must meet, confirmed with your agent.
  • Decking rate per sheet — rotten decking found at tear-off is billed per sheet; the rate should be on the quote, not discovered mid-job.
  • Cosmetic exclusion asked about — if a Class 4 discount is part of your math, ask your insurer whether it carries a cosmetic-damage exclusion.
  • Two local quotes minimum — Texas quotes vary widely by hail history and insurer requirements; one quote is not a market.

Texas roof cost FAQs

Why is Texas cheaper than the South average?

Because the Texas state-average labor multiplier (×0.80) is below the South regional mean (×0.89). The South mean blends higher-wage states with Texas, so a Texas-specific figure lands lower. The gap is $704 on the example roof, all labor.

How is the Texas multiplier calculated?

From BLS OEWS May 2023 mean wages for construction laborers: the Texas state average ratioed to the national mean. All 49 Texas ZIP3 areas in our data carry the state-average value (range ×0.80–×0.82).

Does the Texas estimate include hail or storm costs?

No. The multiplier adjusts labor only; materials, tear-off, and permit are national figures. Storm-related code upgrades or insurer requirements appear in individual quotes — get at least two local Texas quotes before deciding.

Do I need TWIA coverage for my Texas roof?

Only on the coast. TWIA, the state's insurer of last resort for windstorm and hail, covers the 14 first-tier coastal counties plus parts of Harris County east of Highway 146, and only if a private insurer has declined your wind coverage. Inland, a standard homeowners policy normally includes wind and hail.

Is a Class 4 roof worth it in Texas?

In hail-exposed markets — Dallas–Fort Worth, Austin, Houston — usually yes. Class 4 is the top UL 2218 impact rating, costs roughly 10–25% more than standard architectural shingles, and Texas law requires insurers to offer a premium discount for qualifying installations (reported 10–35% on the dwelling portion). Ask your insurer whether the discount carries a cosmetic-damage exclusion before you decide.

Does my city require a permit for a re-roof in Texas?

It depends on your city. Texas adopts the International Residential Code as the municipal baseline, but cities amend and enforce it locally, and there is no single statewide roofing permit rule. Check with your city's permitting office before work starts and get the requirement in writing on the quote.
