Fence Installation Cost

CostMyProject Research Team Prices checked October 2026

Labor is 30–50% of a fence project — and the part of the quote with the most padding. Here's what installers actually charge per linear foot, what gates/permits/terrain add, and how to read a bid like a contractor.

Quote red flags

  • One lump sum, no breakdown. Honest bids show per-foot material + labor, gates, removal, and permit as separate lines. A single number hides padding.
  • Gates priced as plain footage. A gate needs heavier posts, hinges, and latches — if the bid treats 150 LF as all identical, the gate math is buried somewhere.
  • No post spec. "Set posts" means nothing. You want: depth (30+ in), concrete (yes), and spacing (6–8 ft). Shallow posts are how fences lean in year three.
  • Allowances instead of prices. "Removal: allowance $300" means they haven't looked. Get a fixed per-foot removal price.
  • Same-day pressure. "This price is only good today" is a sales tactic, not a material constraint. Real fence pricing doesn't expire overnight.
  • No permit mentioned. If they don't pull one where required, that's your liability when the city notices — or when you sell.
The 3-bid rule, upgraded:
Get three itemized bids, then run the same inputs through our calculator. Any bid more than ~25% above the calculator's range needs an explanation in writing — slope, access, or premium materials are legitimate reasons; vibes are not.

Installation FAQs

How long does fence installation take?

A pro crew typically finishes 150–200 LF in 1–3 days once materials and permits are ready. DIY with a helper: 2–4 weekends. Rocky soil or many gates add days either way.

Should I get a survey before building a fence?

If your property line is uncertain — yes, $200–$1,200 now beats moving a fence later or a neighbor dispute. If you have recent survey pins visible, you're probably fine. When in doubt, talk to the neighbor first; shared fences split costs in many areas.

What does "call 811" do?

It's the free national utility-locate service: they mark buried gas, electric, and telecom lines within a few days. Hitting a line is dangerous and expensive — pros do this reflexively, DIYers skip it most.
