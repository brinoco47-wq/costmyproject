Material-by-material notes

Wood privacy

The default American backyard fence. Pressure-treated pine is cheapest ($18–$30/LF installed); cedar ($25–$45) resists rot naturally and looks better longer. The catch is maintenance: skip staining and a 20-year fence becomes a 12-year fence. In wet climates, set posts in concrete and keep pickets 2 inches off the soil.

Vinyl / PVC

The "buy once" option. Costs ~40% more than wood installed, but 25–30 year life with essentially zero upkeep. Quality varies wildly — cheap vinyl chalks and cracks; look for UV inhibitors and thicker walls. Repairs mean replacing whole panels, not patching.

Chain-link

Cheapest installed and nearly indestructible, but zero privacy (add slats for ~$3–$5/LF more). Galvanized lasts 20–25 years; vinyl-coated (black/green) costs a bit more and looks far better. Best value for large lots and dog runs.

Aluminum ornamental

The pool-code and HOA favorite: elegant, 30+ year life, zero maintenance. Not a privacy fence (it's see-through by design) — privacy inserts exist but push cost toward vinyl. Won't rust like steel or wrought iron.

Composite

Wood look, plastic durability, premium price. 25–30 year life, no staining ever. The newest category — fewer installers, so get multiple bids. Makes sense when you want wood's look with vinyl's maintenance (or lack of it).

Three worked examples

150 LF wood privacy — DIY vs. pro (Texas)

Pro-installed at ~$33/LF (mid-range): $4,975 — done in 2–3 days. DIY materials at ~$20/LF: $3,000 + ~$200 tool rental + 3 weekends of your time. DIY saves ~$1,775 in cash but costs ~60 hours; it's the classic trade, and it's why wood is the DIY king.

80 LF aluminum around a pool (Florida)

Aluminum installed at ~$48/LF (mid-range): 80 LF = $3,840. Add one self-closing, self-latching pool gate (~$400 installed — code-required in most of Florida) and a permit (~$150). All-in: ~$4,390. Pool code usually mandates 4-ft minimum height and specific latch placement — check before you buy.

300 LF chain-link on acreage (Georgia)

Chain-link installed at ~$19/LF: 300 LF = $5,700 — just $19/ft because per-foot cost dominates at scale and there's no removal or slope adder. Add privacy slats ($4/LF = $1,200) if the road is visible. All-in: ~$6,900, the cheapest way to fence a large lot by a wide margin.

Material FAQs

Does a fence increase home value?

A well-built fence adds curb appeal and is a selling point for buyers with kids or dogs, but appraisers rarely credit full cost — expect partial recovery, not profit. Chain-link can hurt perceived value in upscale neighborhoods; wood and vinyl read as upgrades.

Wood vs. vinyl: which is really cheaper?

Over 15 years on a 150-LF fence: wood ~$7,475 (install + staining), vinyl ~$6,900 (install + ~$0 maintenance). Vinyl wins if you stay put; wood wins if you sell within ~7 years, before staining costs pile up. Full math in our head-to-head.

What's the longest-lasting fence material?

Aluminum (30+ years) and vinyl/composite (25–30 years) outlast wood (15–20) and chain-link (20–25) — assuming each gets its (minimal) required care. In coastal salt air, aluminum and vinyl beat everything.

Cedar vs. pressure-treated pine — is cedar worth the premium?

Cedar runs roughly $7–$15 more per linear foot installed than pine, but it resists rot and insects naturally and holds stain 1–2 years longer per cycle. Over a 15-year life with staining every 2–3 years, cedar's lower maintenance roughly offsets its premium — and it looks better doing it. If you'll stain diligently either way, pine is fine; if you're maintenance-lazy, cedar forgives more.

How deep should fence posts be?

One-third of the post's length underground, with a 30-inch minimum for a 6-ft fence — deeper in frost zones, where footings should sit below the local frost line (36–48 inches in the upper Midwest and Northeast). Every post gets concrete, domed at the top to shed water, over a gravel base. Shallow posts are the single most common cause of leaning fences.

Does composite fencing fade?

Early-generation composite faded noticeably; modern capped composite (a polymer shell over the core) holds color far better, though some lightening in the first year is normal. Ask for the manufacturer's fade warranty in writing — reputable lines warrant against excessive fading for 20–25 years. Dark colors fade more visibly than light ones in strong sun.

How much does chain-link with privacy slats cost?

Slats add roughly $3–$5 per linear foot installed on top of the chain-link price — so a 150-LF fence at $19/LF becomes about $2,850 + $600 in slats = ~$3,450. It's the cheapest privacy fence you can buy. The trade-offs: slats fade unevenly, rattle in wind, and add wind load the original posts weren't engineered for — fine for calm areas, questionable on exposed lots.
