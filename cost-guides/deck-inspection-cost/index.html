Deck inspection cost

CostMyProject Research Team Prices checked October 2026

A pro deck inspection costs $150–$400 — cheap insurance against the most failure-prone structure attached to your house. Here's what the money buys, when you need one, and what the findings typically cost to fix.

What a deck inspector actually checks

Decks fail in predictable places, and a good inspector works through them in order of danger — not convenience. The single most important check is the ledger board connection: the board that ties the deck to your house. Ledger failures cause the catastrophic collapses you read about, and they're invisible from the top of the deck. Everything else is secondary to that one connection.

Ledger boardBolt/lag pattern, flashing, rot behind the board#1 deck failure point — a failed ledger drops the whole deck

When you actually need a deck inspection

  • Buying or selling a home. This is the #1 reason. A deck with structural issues can kill a deal or become a $5,000+ credit negotiation — a $250 inspection before listing (or before waiving contingencies) is the cheapest leverage in the transaction.
  • Every 3–5 years on wood decks. Pressure-treated decks age invisibly from the top. The framing underneath is where the clock runs out, and 10–15 years is a typical structural lifespan for PT framing in wet climates.
  • After major storms. High winds rack the framing; heavy snow overloads it; flooding undermines footings. If the deck shifted, leans, or the stairs pulled away even slightly, get it checked before the next season loads it again.
  • Before refinishing or resurfacing. Spending $3,000+ to resurface a deck with a rotting ledger is lighting money on fire. Inspect first, then decide whether the structure deserves the new surface.
  • When something feels wrong. Bouncy boards, a railing that wobbles, stairs that creak under load, or visible rot anywhere — these are the deck telling you its inspection is overdue.
The ledger rule If your deck is more than 10 years old and you've never verified how the ledger board is attached, that single check is worth the entire inspection fee. Decks built before modern flashing and bolting standards are the ones that fail — and the failure mode is sudden, not gradual.

DIY deck check vs. hiring a pro

A homeowner walkthrough catches surface problems. It cannot verify structural adequacy — and it's the structure that kills. Here's the honest split:

CheckDIY (free)Pro ($150–$400)
Rot probe (screwdriver test on boards & posts)✅ Easy — soft wood tells you plenty✅ Included
Loose boards, popped nails, wobbly railings✅ Easy to spot✅ Included
Ledger bolt pattern & flashing condition⚠️ Hard to access, easy to misread✅ Core of the inspection
Joist span & load adequacy❌ Requires span tables + framing knowledge✅ Measured and verified
Footing depth & soil conditions❌ Buried — you can't see it✅ Probed and assessed
Written report for a sale or insurance❌ Your notes carry no weight✅ The deliverable you're paying for

Do the DIY check every spring — it takes 20 minutes and catches the cheap problems early. But treat it as triage, not a verdict. If your DIY check finds rot, movement, or anything structural-looking, that's when the $250 pro inspection earns its keep. And if you're buying a house with a deck, skip straight to the pro: your 20-minute walkthrough is not due diligence.

Repair ranges are contractor-reported planning figures. The pattern: surface fixes are hundreds, structural fixes are thousands, and a failed structure means pricing a new deck — run your numbers through the deck cost calculator before deciding between repair and rebuild.

Notice the economics: the inspection ($150–$400) is 2–5% of a typical structural repair and under 2% of a rebuild. Its real value isn't the checklist — it's the repair-vs-rebuild decision. A deck with a bad ledger and good everything else is a $2,500 repair. A deck with a bad ledger, rotted joists, and settling footings is a rebuild candidate, and you want to know that before you pay for resurfacing.

How to choose a deck inspector

  • Credentials matter more than price. A licensed home inspector with structural experience or a structural engineer is worth more than the cheapest option. For pre-purchase inspections, many buyers use their home inspector's deck add-on ($75–$150 extra) — fine for screening, but get the standalone structural inspection if anything looks off.
  • Ask what's in the report. Verbal-only findings are worthless in a real-estate negotiation. Insist on a written report with photos and prioritized recommendations.
  • Beware the free inspection. Deck builders offering "free inspections" are selling rebuilds. An independent inspector with no repair business has no incentive to find problems that don't exist.
  • Check insurance. The inspector should carry errors-and-omissions insurance — if they miss a failed ledger, you want recourse.

FAQs

How much does a deck inspection cost?

$150–$400 for a professional inspection in 2026 (industry estimates). Basic visual inspections run $150–$250; detailed structural inspections with a written report run $250–$400. Large or multi-level decks cost more.

What does a deck inspector check?

The ledger board connection to the house, flashing, joists and beams, posts and footings, decking boards, railings, stairs, and all fasteners — plus rot and insect damage throughout. The ledger connection is the #1 failure point and the most important check.

How often should a deck be inspected?

Every 3–5 years for wood decks, after major storms, when buying or selling a home, and before any major refinishing project. Composite decks need less frequent checks but the framing underneath ages the same way.

Can I inspect my own deck?

You can do a useful 20-minute visual check for rot, loose boards, and wobbly railings — but you can't verify the ledger connection, joist spans, or footing depth yourself. Hire a pro before buying a home or if the deck is over 10 years old.

What happens if a deck fails inspection?

You get a prioritized repair list. Minor fixes run $200–$800; structural repairs $1,500–$8,000+; a failed structure means pricing a rebuild ($8,000–$25,000+). On a home sale, findings typically become a repair credit or seller repair.
